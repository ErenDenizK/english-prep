# What the boundaries actually are

2026-09-06. A research arm for App 2. `docs/business/vision.md` §3 settles
the unit — a **boundary**, a pair or small set the learner's ear conflates —
and settles that progress is *boundaries closed*, which is finite and
countable. It does not say what they are. This document tries to produce
the inventory, and to say honestly how much of the product's spine survives
contact with the evidence.

Nothing here re-argues §3. The three questions it does try to answer are:
what is on the list, what shape are the things on it, and how long is the
list.

**Evidence marking.** `[S]` a search summary — the finding reached me
through a search result, not by reading the source. `[?]` unverified, stated
because it is load-bearing and someone should check it. `[≈]` my own
reasoning over the material. There is no `[R]`: WebFetch and curl are
blocked in this session, so **nothing below was read at source**, and every
number that matters is `[S]` at best. Treat the frequency column as
ordering, not measurement.

## The answer in six sentences

**The inventory is real and it is 110 items long** (§3), of which 64 are
genuinely binary, 30 are three-or-more-way sets, and 16 **are not
contrast-shaped at all** — but those 16 include the single largest error
categories in every learner corpus, so counting them as 15% of the list
understates them badly as a share of the errors learners actually make. That last group is the
finding: articles, dependent prepositions, countability, collocation and
inflectional -s are individually enormous error categories, they are what
learner corpora actually count, and none of them is a boundary between two
things. The unit is not falsified by this, because **App 2's audience is not
the population those corpora sample** — a learner who acquired English from
series and games already produces third-person -s and rarely omits articles;
what they smear is exactly the paired stuff. But that defence is a claim
about the audience, not about English, and it is testable in v0.1 rather
than assumable. On the denominator: the honest answer is **finite but only
because we choose a resolution** — Cambridge's English Grammar Profile
enumerates 1,200+ grammar competences `[S]` and Swan's *Practical English
Usage* 600+ entries `[S]`, so "how many boundaries exist" has no natural
answer, and the number the product ships is a curatorial decision that must
be defended as one. And the sharpest editorial finding is §5's: **most of
the classic boundary syllabus is exam and writing furniture** for a learner
who already functions, and the list that would actually change how such a
person is heard is much shorter than the list a grammar book contains.

## 1 · Where these came from, and what that is worth

Four bodies of evidence, in descending order of how much I trust them for
this purpose.

**Error-tagged learner corpora and the GEC shared tasks.** The Cambridge
Learner Corpus is ~16 million words of exam scripts from learners with 86
different mother tongues, errors retained and coded by a two-letter scheme
where the first letter is the error operation and the second the word class
`[S]`. EFCAMDAT is larger and freer — 70M+ words, 1.2M essays, ~175,000
learners across the CEFR range `[S]`. On top of these sit the annotated sets
the grammatical-error-correction field uses: FCE, NUCLE, Lang-8,
W&I+LOCNESS, retagged by ERRANT `[S]`. These are the only sources here that
produce counts rather than opinions.

What they agree on, across every framing I could find: the largest error
categories are **punctuation, spelling, determiners/articles, prepositions,
noun number, verb tense and form, and word choice** `[S]`. NUCLE's own
documentation names article-or-determiner (`ArtOrDet`) and noun number
(`Nn`) as among the most common types in its development set `[S]`. Studies
outside the shared tasks put lexical errors — collocation and word choice
plus spelling — at around 15% of all errors in one EFL corpus, and verb
choice alone at ~13% in another `[S]`. Operation-level splits vary wildly
between studies (one reports misformation 50% / omission 30%; another
omission 62% / addition 18%) `[S]`, which is itself informative: **these
taxonomies are not comparable across papers**, so any attempt to build a
priority list by adding up published percentages is building on sand `[≈]`.

**The reference-grammar tradition.** Swan's *Practical English Usage* is
organised as 600+ entries answering "common questions learners ask" `[S]`;
Murphy's *English Grammar in Use* is 145 units, *Advanced Grammar in Use*
100–120 depending on edition `[S]`. These are not evidence about learners,
they are a century of teachers' accumulated judgement about where the
trouble is — which is a different and still useful thing, and it is the
tradition App 1's own 60 categories descend from.

**L1-contrast reference work.** Swan & Smith's *Learner English* is the
standard: 22 chapters, one per language background, including a dedicated
Turkish chapter `[S]`. It is the source that lets you say *which* boundaries
a given L1 makes harder, and it is exactly the resource App 2 gives up by
having no known L1.

**Single-L1 error-analysis papers.** Numerous, small, methodologically
uneven, and useful mainly as convergent evidence when several independent
ones name the same thing. I leaned on them for §4.

**What is missing, and it matters.** I could find no corpus study of the
specific population this product is for — **naturalistic or extramural
acquirers of English who never had formal instruction.** The extramural-
English literature is about vocabulary uptake and comprehension from
subtitled TV and gaming `[S]`, not about what such learners get wrong.
Everything in learner-corpus research samples *instructed* learners, mostly
through exam scripts and classroom essays. So the inventory below is
**derived from the wrong population and corrected by argument**, and §7 is
where I say what that could cost.

## 2 · Three shapes, and the one that is a problem

The brief asks for the split, so here it is first, because it changes how to
read the table.

**(a) Genuinely binary.** Two forms, one dimension of difference, and the
learner's job is to locate a line. *must / have to*. *few / a few*. *since /
for*. These are what the unit was designed for. The lesson writes itself
because the contrast *is* the content, and — important for this project —
the item is authorable without an absolute rule, because you can build the
sentence so that only one side fits and still admit the other exists.

**(b) Three-or-more-way sets.** *must / can't / might* for deduction.
*much / many / a lot of / plenty of*. *for example / for instance / such as
/ namely*. These are still contrast-shaped, but they behave differently in
three ways `[≈]`: the sets are frequently *asymmetric* (one member is the
default and the others are marked), a four-option question can only test one
edge of the set at a time so a "closed" set needs more items than a pair,
and the set membership itself is often arguable — which is the project's own
"an option a competent teacher would accept is a wrong option" problem,
arriving at authoring time rather than review time. App 1 already carries
plenty of these: `Much vs Many vs A Lot Of vs Plenty Of` is a four-way and
`Modal Perfects: Must / Can't / Should / Needn't Have` is a four-way whose
members do not even share a dimension.

**(c) Not boundaries at all.** A single fact the learner does not have.
*depend* takes *on*. *advice* is uncountable. *rely on* occurs with that
preposition in ~99.5% of its uses `[S]`. There is nothing to contrast: the
research is explicit that **"there is no logical system underlying which
preposition goes with which word"** `[S]`, which is the cleanest statement I
found of why this category resists the unit. You can force such a fact into
a pair — *depend on* vs *depend of* — but that is a fake contrast, because
*depend of* is not a thing a competent speaker would ever accept, and the
whole design of this project's items is built on distractors that a
competent speaker *would* accept being disqualifying.

**How much of the real error mass is (c)?** For instructed learners in exam
conditions: a great deal. Articles, spelling, punctuation, noun number and
preposition selection between them dominate every corpus count I found
`[S]`, and all five are (c)-shaped or nearly so. If App 2's audience looked
like a learner corpus, **the unit choice would be in serious trouble** and I
would be saying so as the headline.

**Why I do not think it is, and how thin that is.** The audience is defined
as someone whose ear is good — they picked English up from series, films and
games and they already function. Three things follow `[≈]`. First, the (c)
categories that dominate corpora are precisely the ones **high-frequency
input teaches for free**: nobody who has watched a thousand hours of English
television omits articles or third-person -s, because those are the most
frequent, most audible things in the stream. Second, the errors that survive
massive input are the ones input **does not disambiguate**, and a contrast
is exactly a case where two forms occur in similar contexts and the
distinction is semantic rather than distributional — which is why the
fossilization literature keeps finding persistent non-target features in
long-term naturalistic L2 users after decades of exposure `[S]`. Third, the
(c) items that *do* survive — dependent prepositions, countability of
abstract nouns, collocation — survive because they are **low-frequency
lexical facts**, and those are a vocabulary product, not a grammar one.

So the honest position: **the boundary unit is right for the audience and
wrong for the population that has been measured**, and the one cheap
experiment that separates those is the v0.1 diagnostic. If it comes back
showing that the people who show up are dropping articles, the unit is not
the problem — the audience statement is, and it is the audience statement
that would need to move.

**One correction to §3's own example.** *wrote / written* is listed there as
a boundary and it is a good instinct but a mixed case `[≈]`. Two things are
going on: *which slot takes V2 and which takes V3* is a real binary and a
teachable one; *the past participle of "write" is "written"* is a fact with
no contrast in it. The first is a boundary, the second is a lookup. Worth
splitting when it is authored, because the lesson for one is nothing like
the lesson for the other.

Read that split carefully, because it flatters the unit. 16 of 110 sounds
like a rounding error; those 16 are articles, countability, subject-verb

and punctuation, which is very close to a list of **the largest error
categories every corpus in §1 reports** `[S]`. By item count the inventory
is 85% boundaries. By error mass over an instructed population it is
probably the other way round `[≈]`. Both numbers are true and only one of
them is about App 2's audience, which is the whole of §2's argument and the
whole of §7's doubt about it.

I have also not padded the (c) count downwards: it would be easy to
reclassify articles as *a/an vs the* and gerund-vs-infinitive as *remember
to vs remember -ing* and call them boundaries, and that is exactly the
self-deception this section exists to prevent.

## 4 · Near-universal versus L1-specific

App 2 has no known L1, so this table is not a targeting tool — it is a
**prior for the diagnostic**. The near-universal rows are worth building
first because every learner meets them; the L1-specific rows are worth
*detecting*, because a learner who fails one is telling you something about
themselves that no other signal in the product will.

**Near-universal — reported for essentially every L1 I searched** `[≈]` over
`[S]` sources:

- Present perfect vs past simple (1), and *since / for* (10). Named for
  Spanish `[S]`, German `[S]`, Slavic `[S]`, and it is the single most
  recurrent item in this document.
- *must / have to* and *mustn't / don't have to* (19, 20). Deontic source
  and negation scope are not marked the same way in any L1 I looked at.
- Gerund vs infinitive (52). Lexical, so no L1 gets it free.
- Dependent prepositions (95). Lexical for the same reason; named as a
  major error source "for even advanced learners" `[S]`.
- Conditionals 2 vs 3 (32) and *wish* (36).
- -ed/-ing adjectives (103) `[S]`.
- Phrasal-verb avoidance (100) — documented across Arabic, Chinese, Dutch,
  Hebrew, Japanese, Korean, Persian, Portuguese, Spanish and Swedish `[S]`,
  which is about as close to universal as this document gets, with the
  caveat that Germanic-L1 learners avoid *less* `[S]`.

**Strongly L1-specific:**

| Boundary | Hard for | Easy for | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Articles (38) | Mandarin, Japanese, Korean, Russian, Polish, Turkish — no article system | Spanish, French, German, Greek, Arabic (partly) | Spanish speakers' article accuracy rises with proficiency while Russian speakers' does not `[S]`; Slavic learners reportedly ~90% at C1, ~99% at C2 `[S]` |
| Definite-article *over*use (38) | Arabic — no indefinite article, and *al-* attaches more widely than *the* | — | `[S]` |
| Countability (40) | Mandarin, Japanese — no count/mass grammar; *scenery*, *news*, *advice* are countable in the L1 | — | context-dependent errors >60% of countability errors in one Japanese study `[S]` |
| Subject–verb agreement, tense inflection, plural -s (86, 92) | Mandarin — no inflection or agreement at all | Romance, Slavic, Turkish | `[S]`; post-puberty L1-Chinese learners predicted to struggle with inflectional morphology `[S]` |
| Perfect/progressive vs perfective/imperfective (1, 2, 4) | Slavic — the L1 grammaticalises a *different* aspect distinction, so the confusion is systematic rather than random | Romance (partly — Spanish has its own present-perfect mismatch `[S]`) | L1-Slavic speakers associate English past simple with completion in processing studies `[S]` |
| Present tense with *since* (17) | German (*seit* + present) `[S]`, Spanish (*desde* + present) `[S]` | — | `[S]` |
| Resumptive pronouns in relatives (73) | Arabic — the L1 requires the pronoun English forbids | — | `[S]` |
| Copula omission | Arabic — no present-tense copula | — | `[S]` |
| Adverb placement (109) | French — adverbs sit between verb and object | — | `[S]` |
| False friends (L-group) | Romance and Germanic L1s, *specifically because* they share vocabulary with English | Mandarin, Japanese, Turkish — no shared stock to mislead them | `[S]` |
| Prepositions (93–95) | Turkish — case suffixes rather than prepositions; reported as the **most common** error type for Turkish learners `[S]` | — | `[S]` |
| SOV → SVO word order, zero relatives | Turkish, Japanese, Korean | — | `[S]` |

**The design consequence.** Note the shape of that table: **almost every
L1-specific row is (c)-shaped and almost every near-universal row is
(a)-shaped** `[≈]`. That is a genuinely convenient finding and I distrust it
slightly for that reason — but it has a plain mechanism behind it. L1
transfer bites hardest where English marks something morphologically that
the L1 does not mark at all (articles, agreement, plural, case-as-
preposition), and those are facts, not contrasts. Contrasts are hard for
everyone because they are semantic distinctions inside English, and no L1
hands you those.

**So: build the (a) near-universals, and use the (c) L1-specifics as
diagnostics rather than lessons.** A learner who drops articles has told you
their L1 family in one item. Whether App 2 should *do* anything with that —
given that it deliberately gave up the L1 shortcut — is a product question,
and the honest answer from §3 of the vision is that it should measure, not
label.

## 5 · What is worth teaching to someone who is not preparing for anything

This is the section I would most want argued with, because it cuts the
inventory hardest, and because it is where the corpora are least use: exam
scripts are the wrong evidence for "what matters when nobody is marking
you."

Three tests, applied in order `[≈]`:

1. **Would getting this wrong change what a listener understands?**
2. **Would getting this wrong mark the speaker as a non-native in a way they
   would mind?**
3. **Does the learner's ear already produce the right answer most of the
   time, so that naming the boundary is a genuine refinement rather than a
   repair?**

Test 3 is the audience test from `CLAUDE.md`, and it is the one that
reorders everything. Anything failing it is teaching from zero, which is
somebody else's product.

**Tier 1 — changes meaning. Teach these first.** These are boundaries where
the wrong choice does not sound foreign, it says something else, and the
listener has no way to detect the error.

- 20 *mustn't / don't have to* — forbidden vs unnecessary. The most
  meaning-critical pair in the whole inventory `[≈]`.
- 53 *stop/remember/forget/try + -ing vs to* — reverses the meaning
  outright.
- 12 *been to / gone to* — the person is either here or not.
- 41/42 *few / a few*, *little / a little* — flips the attitude.
- 25/26 *must / can't / might* — the strength of a claim.
- 34 *unless* vs *if not*.
- 15 *by / until* — a deadline that is not one.
- 44 *fewer / less* fails this test; 62 *have something done* passes it.
- 64 *say / tell*, 96 *to / for* — recipient roles.
- Several L-group pairs: *lend/borrow*, *bring/take*, *remember/remind*,
  *learn/teach*. These are meaning-critical and they are also the ones a
  series-and-games learner is *most* likely to have right already, so they
  score high on 1 and 2 and low on 3.

**Tier 2 — marks the speaker, does not obscure the meaning.** Worth
teaching, second.

- 1 present perfect vs past simple. Nearly always recoverable from context,
  and nearly always noticed.
- 3 stative verbs with -ing ("I'm knowing").
- 103 *bored / boring* — a classic, comprehensible, and instantly marking.
- 95 dependent prepositions — the research says these "mark writing as
  non-native and are hard to correct without deliberate practice" `[S]`,
  which is precisely test 2 and precisely not test 1.
- 19 *must / have to*, 22 *had better*, 27 *should have*.
- 109 adverb placement.
- 100 phrasal verb vs Latinate — a register mark in both directions, and the
  one place where a series-and-games learner is likely to be *over*-informal
  rather than under.

**Tier 3 — writing and exams only.** Real boundaries, correctly described in
every grammar book, and largely irrelevant to a person who is not being
assessed `[≈]`:

- 68 *who / whom* — whom is receding in speech `[S]`; teaching it as a
  boundary to a functioning speaker is teaching a shibboleth.
- 67's *that / which* rule — a written-register convention, contested even
  among native editors `[?]`.
- 70/71 preposition + relative pronoun, reduced relatives — academic prose.
- 63 *It is said that / is said to* — this is in App 1 because the İYS
  restatement section rewards it. It has nothing to do with functioning in
  English.
- 79/80/81 the formal connector inventory — *moreover*, *namely*,
  *nevertheless*. These exist because essays are graded.
- 84 comma splices, 105's *than/then*, 108's *affect/effect* — orthography.
- 18 future perfect, 37 *as if* + past, 59 *of/for + to*.

**The honest count.** Of 110 candidates I would put **roughly 25 in Tier 1,
roughly 35 in Tier 2, and roughly 50 in Tier 3** `[≈]`. That is a startling
result and it should be checked before it is trusted, but if it survives it
is the most commercially significant line in this document: **App 2's real
spine is about 60 boundaries, not 110**, and the other 50 are the reason
grammar books are long. Vision §3's v1.0 target of "~100 boundaries" is
therefore either right for the wrong reason — it reaches 100 by including
Tier 3 — or it is right for a version of App 2 that has quietly become an
exam product again.

**And one thing missing from every tier.** Nothing in this inventory
addresses the biggest actual weakness of a series-and-games learner, which
is `[≈]` **register**: knowing that *gonna*, *kinda*, *stuff like that* and
the phrasal-verb-heavy style they absorbed are a dialect of English, not
English. That is not a boundary between two forms; it is a boundary between
two *situations*, and if App 2 wants one genuinely differentiated feature,
"the same sentence, said two ways, and when each is right" is the one this
research would nominate. It fits the unit — it is a contrast — and it is
almost absent from grammar syllabuses because grammar syllabuses assume the
learner arrived through school.

## 6 · The denominator, which is the part that does not work

The product claims "N boundaries, you have closed 43." That claim needs N to
exist. Here is what the evidence says about N, in the order the numbers get
worse.

**The optimistic anchors.** Murphy's *English Grammar in Use* is 145 units,
*Advanced Grammar in Use* 100–120 `[S]`. App 1's own taxonomy is 60
categories over 10 topics. My inventory is 110. These cluster around
**100–150**, which is a very comfortable number for a product, and it is the
number I would have produced if I had only consulted the teaching
tradition.

**The pessimistic anchors.** Swan's *Practical English Usage* is 600+
entries `[S]` — and it is organised precisely around "problems", which is
the same construct as a boundary. Cambridge's English Grammar Profile,
derived from 55M+ words of learner data, enumerates **over 1,200 grammatical
competence statements** across A1–C2 `[S]`. That is not a syllabus; it is an
attempt to be exhaustive about English grammar as learners actually acquire
it, and it is an order of magnitude above the comfortable number.

**Why the two disagree, and which one is right.** They are measuring
different things, and neither is wrong `[≈]`. 145 is *how many lessons a
book has room for*. 1,200 is *how many separable grammatical competences
there are*. The gap between them is entirely **resolution**: "conditionals"
is one Murphy chapter, several App 1 categories, a dozen EGP statements and
an unbounded number of actual sentence patterns. There is no fact of the
matter about which resolution is correct. **The number of boundaries in
English is not a property of English; it is a property of how finely you
choose to cut it.**

**And the lexical class has no edge at all.** Group L is genuinely
unbounded. *make/do*, *lend/borrow* and *affect/effect* are the famous ones
because they are frequent, but confusable pairs continue at diminishing
frequency indefinitely, and collocation — *strong tea*, *heavy rain*, and
the finding that some prepositional verbs are 99.5%-fixed `[S]` — is a fact
per lexical item across the whole vocabulary. You cannot count that to a
finish. Any published N is a count of the *grammatical* boundaries with the
lexical ones excluded or sampled, and the product will need to say so.

**So what do I actually tell the owner?**

The claim "done is finite and countable" is **true in the only sense a
product can deliver and false in the sense a learner might hear it** `[≈]`.
It is true that "there are 110 boundaries in this app and you have closed
43" — that is a statement about the corpus, it is honest, it is checkable,
and it is exactly the kind of thing this project is good at. It is not true
that closing them means English is finished, and the reason the distinction
matters here more than it would elsewhere is that **this product's entire
differentiation is refusing to overstate things.** A learner who closes 110
boundaries and then discovers boundary 111 has been sold a small lie by a
product whose pitch was not lying.

The fix is a sentence, not a redesign, and it is a sentence this project is
temperamentally suited to writing: *these are the boundaries in this app,
there are this many, and when they are closed I will tell you and stop.*
Vision §3's third notification — "You have closed everything currently here.
I will stop reminding you until there is something new" — already says
almost exactly this, and it is more defensible than the counter it sits
next to. **The countable thing is the corpus, not the language.** If the
product says that plainly, the design claim survives §6 intact. If it lets
"N boundaries" be heard as "all of English's boundaries", §6 falsifies it.

**One more consequence, on supply.** If N is a curatorial choice, then
growing the corpus is not "finishing English", it is **raising the
resolution**, and that is an infinitely available supply at the same 6–8
minutes of the owner's attention per item. That is good news for the
subscription tension in vision §3 and bad news for the promise of finishing.
Both are true, and choosing between them is the same values decision the
pricing section already identified.

## 7 · Where I would be wrong

**The population problem, which is the big one.** Every count in §1 comes
from instructed learners writing exam scripts and classroom essays. App 2's
audience is defined as the opposite: naturalistic acquirers with a good ear
and no schooling in the language. I could find **no error-analysis corpus of
that population at all** — the extramural-English literature studies uptake,
not error `[S]`. My §2 defence of the boundary unit rests entirely on an
argument (high-frequency input teaches the (c) categories for free) that I
could not find measured. **If that argument is wrong, §2's (c) group is not
16 of 110 but the majority of what App 2's users actually need, and the unit
is the wrong unit.** The v0.1 diagnostic is a cheap test of exactly this and
should be designed to answer it — which means including a few deliberately
(c)-shaped items (an article slot, a plural, a dependent preposition) not
because they are boundaries but because **their failure rate is the
falsification test for the whole product's spine.**

**The frequency column is soft.** It is my synthesis of how often something
appeared in search summaries, not a count. Search results are biased towards
what ELT blogs write about, and ELT blogs write about *must/have to* because
it is teachable, not because it is frequent. A reviewer with access to the
BEA-2019 error distribution table or the CLC error codes would reorder this
inventory and should.

**Tier 3 might be Tier 1 for the users who actually arrive.** §5 assumes
nobody is preparing for anything. But the most motivated audience for a
grammar-refinement app is plausibly people who *are* — IELTS candidates,
people writing in English at work, graduate students. If App 2's real users
turn out to be those people, §5's cut is exactly backwards and the exam
furniture is the product. This is measurable from what people use, not
arguable in advance, and it would be an argument for keeping Tier 3 authored
but unpromoted rather than dropping it.

**I may be too confident that (b) sets are fine.** I treated three-way sets
as a mild variant of a boundary. It is possible they are a different unit
that happens to look similar — four-option items over a four-member set can
only ever test one pairwise edge, so "closing" a set may need six items
where a pair needs two, and the product's counting model treats them as one
each. That is a content-cost error of up to 3× on a quarter of the
inventory, and App 1 has enough four-member categories to check it against
real data rather than guess.

**The register finding at the end of §5 is my own and unsupported.** No
source told me that series-and-games learners are over-informal. It follows
from what they learned from, and it matches the phrasal-verb literature in
the opposite direction — those learners should *over*-use phrasal verbs
where the corpora show instructed learners avoiding them `[S]`. That is a
sharp, falsifiable prediction and it would be a good second question for the
diagnostic. But it is a prediction, not a finding.

**And the thing I could not do.** Everything above is search summaries. The
five sources that would settle most of this — the BEA-2019 error-type table,
the CLC error-code frequencies, Swan & Smith's per-language chapters, the
EGP statement list, and any corpus of naturalistic acquirers if one exists —
all need to be read rather than searched. If one arm of follow-up work is
funded, it is reading the EGP list, because it is the only document that
would replace §6's estimate with a number.
