## The one fact the whole topic is built on

Turkish has no relative pronoun. It builds the same clause with a
**participle placed before the noun** — *okuduğum kitap*, *tavanı çöken
sinema*, *çalıştığım şirket* — and marks the relation with a suffix on
the verb, not with a word standing between the two clauses. Three
consequences, and every misconception below is one of them:

1. **There is nothing to choose.** Turkish never asks *who* or *which* or
   *whose*; the participle is the same whatever the noun is and whatever
   role it plays. So the learner has no native intuition for the choice
   and reaches for whichever pronoun the last teacher said most often.
2. **There is no comma distinction.** *Kardeşim, Ankara'da oturan* and
   *Ankara'da oturan kardeşim* are not a defining/non-defining pair; the
   comma is punctuation, not grammar. A learner cannot *hear* the
   difference an English comma makes, which is why **any item this app
   keys on defining-versus-non-defining must print the comma**.
3. **The preposition has nowhere to strand.** Turkish uses a case suffix
   inside the participle (*çalıştığım şirket*), so `in which` /
   `for whom` / `at which` have no shape to copy, and the learner either
   drops the preposition entirely or attaches it to `that`.

The topic-wide honest bound, from the kickoff: in everyday English `whom`
is receding and `that` freely replaces `which` in defining clauses. Every
item below is keyed only where the formal rule **genuinely decides** — a
printed comma, a preposition standing in front of the blank, a mixed
antecedent, a missing subject — never on which of two accepted forms
sounds more exam-like. Where a category could not honestly key its own
headline contrast, §1 says so.

Shared misconceptions, cited by number from more than one category:

  MX1. English relative pronouns are stylistic variants of one another,
       so any of them will do → the learner picks by sound.
  MX2. `what` is the general-purpose relative pronoun (from *ne / şey*)
       → *all what I found*, *the reason what*.
  MX3. Two clauses may be joined by a comma and a pronoun, as in Turkish
       → *nine samples, some of them were untested*.

# 1 · `Who vs Whom vs Whose`

## 1 · The discrimination

Can decide a relative pronoun by the role it plays **inside its own
clause** — subject, object, or possessor — rather than by the noun in
front of it: sees that the slot after the blank is a bare verb (subject →
`who`), a noun (possessor → `whose`), or a subject-plus-verb with a gap
(object → `whom`).

**Honest bound.** This category cannot discriminate object `who` from
object `whom` in a defining clause. *The colleagues who she trained* is
accepted by competent speakers everywhere outside a style guide, so no
item keys `whom` against `who` in that environment — and item 4, which
does key `whom`, does it in a **non-defining** clause with `who` absent
from the options, so the item turns on what genuinely cannot fill the
slot (`that` after a comma, `which` for a person, `whose` without a
following noun) rather than on a preference. `whom` against `who` after a
preposition is real and undisputed, and it lives in category 4, where the
preposition is printed. Nor may an item key `who` against `that` in a
defining clause: both are correct, so `that` is never an option here.

## 2 · Misconceptions

  M1. `whom` is the polite/exam form of `who`, so it is safer → chosen in
      subject position (*the engineer whom repaired the lamp*).
  M2. `whose` and `who` are interchangeable before a word that could be
      either a noun or a verb → *the analyst who reports* where the
      sentence needs *whose reports*.
  M3. `who's` is the possessive, by analogy with the English genitive
      apostrophe the learner met first → *the analyst who's reports*.
  M4. `whose` is only for people, because possession is imagined as
      animate → *the cinema which ceiling collapsed*.
  M5. A comma changes nothing (fact 2), so `that` is available everywhere
      → *a drummer, that the jury had heard once*.
  M6. `which` may refer to a person, since Turkish has one participle for
      both.

## 3 · The item plan

| # | keys | tests | decided by | misconceptions punished |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | `who` | subject role | signal (bare verb after the blank) | M1, M4, M6 |
| 2 | `whose` | possessor, human, where the following word could be a verb | **meaning** (number agreement of the main verb, three words later) | M2, M3 |
| 3 | `whose` | possessor, **non-human** — the caveat | meaning (what belongs to what) | M4, M6 |
| 4 | `whom` | object role in a non-defining clause | signal (printed comma + filled subject slot) | M5, M6 |

Both halves keyed: subject once, possessor twice, object once. Item 2 is
the meaning-decided one — `who reports` and `whose reports` are both
possible until the main verb `are quoted` demands a plural subject. Item
3 is the caveat item: the lesson's emphatic block is *`whose` is not only
for people*, and nothing else in the set springs it.

## 4 · Context bank

1. A retired lighthouse keeper and a missing maintenance record.
2. A subscription newsletter by an analyst nobody has met.
3. A 1930s cinema with a collapsed ceiling.
4. A jazz drummer who wins a prize on one recording.
5. *(spare)* A ferry captain and a rescued dog.
6. *(spare)* A bookbinder and an unsigned diary.

One per item, none shared, none from a lesson.

## 5 · Difficulty recipe

1. The word after the blank is ambiguous between a noun and a verb, and
   the agreement that resolves it sits later in the sentence.
2. The possessor is inanimate, so M4 fires.
3. The clause is non-defining, so the comma — not the pronoun — is the
   evidence.

Not wanted: an object `whom` argued against an acceptable `who`; a rare
noun; `that` offered where it would also be correct.

# 2 · `Defining vs Non-defining: That vs Which`

## 1 · The discrimination

Can tell a clause that **identifies** its noun from one that **adds
information** to a noun already identified, and knows what follows from
it: no commas and `that`/`which` both available in the first, commas and
`which` only in the second.

**Honest bound.** In a defining clause `that` and `which` are equally
correct, so **no item is keyed on that contrast**. An item may only be
keyed where one of the two is genuinely impossible:

- after a comma, `that` cannot occur (items 1, 3);
- with an antecedent that is people **and** things, `which` cannot occur
  (item 2);
- after the fused antecedent `all`, `which` cannot occur (item 4).

The superlative/`the only` preference (*the best film that I have seen*)
is a preference, not a rule, and is not keyed anywhere. Nor is the
omission of the pronoun, which is always available in an object slot and
would be a second defensible answer.

## 2 · Misconceptions

  M7. A comma is punctuation, not grammar (fact 2) → `that` used after
      one.
  M8. The pronoun agrees with the **nearest** noun rather than with the
      whole antecedent → `which` after *the volunteers and the equipment*.
  M9. A relative pronoun always replaces a noun, never a whole clause →
      `that`/`this` where sentential `which` is needed.
  M10. Two clauses may be joined by a comma and a demonstrative (MX3) →
      *…, this meant the dough was wasted*.
  M11. `that` and `which` are interchangeable everywhere, including after
      `all`, `everything`, `the only thing`.
  M12. `what` is the general relative pronoun (MX2) → *all what the team
      found*.

## 3 · The item plan

| # | keys | tests | decided by | misconceptions punished |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | `which` | non-defining, printed commas, ordinary noun | signal (the commas) | M7, M12 |
| 2 | `that` | defining, mixed human + non-human antecedent | **meaning** (what the antecedent actually is) | M8 |
| 3 | `which` | sentential — the antecedent is the whole previous clause | **meaning** (there is no single noun it can attach to) | M9, M10, M12 |
| 4 | `that` | fused antecedent `all` | signal (`All` opens the sentence) | M11, M12 |

Two `which`, two `that`: guessing the majority form scores 50%. Items 2
and 3 are decided by meaning — in 3 the nearest noun (*the ovens*) is a
perfectly good decoy antecedent, and only the sense rules it out. Item 4
is the caveat item: it springs the rule the rest of the category teaches
(*they are interchangeable in defining clauses*) in the one place it
fails.

**Every item in this category prints the comma when a comma decides it.**

## 4 · Context bank

1. A beekeeper's inherited hive on a hillside.
2. Volunteers and timing equipment stranded before a road race.
3. A bakery losing a morning's dough to a power cut.
4. A three-summer excavation that finds almost nothing.
5. *(spare)* A ship's bell returned to a maritime museum.
6. *(spare)* A translated novel and its first reviewer.

## 5 · Difficulty recipe

1. The sentential antecedent, with a plausible single-noun decoy in front
   of it.
2. A compound antecedent that spans the animacy line.
3. The non-defining reading is forced by the world (only one such thing
   exists), not only by the comma.

Not wanted: keying defining `that` against defining `which`; an item
where the comma is described in the explanation but not printed in the
paragraph; `who` offered where `that` would also serve.

# 3 · `Where vs When vs Why vs Which`

## 1 · The discrimination

Can tell a relative **adverb** from a relative **pronoun**: reads the
clause after the blank and asks whether anything is missing from it. If
the clause is complete, the blank stands for an adverbial (*in that
craft*, *in that summer*, *for that reason*) and takes `where` / `when` /
`why`; if a subject or object is missing, it takes `which`.

**Honest bound.** `the summer when` / `the summer that` / `the summer ∅`
and `the reason why` / `the reason that` / `the reason ∅` are all
correct, so **`that` is never offered in this category** — it would be a
second defensible answer in three of the four items. Nor may an item be
keyed on `where` against `in which`: they are interchangeable, and that
contrast belongs to category 4, where the preposition is printed.

## 2 · Misconceptions

  M13. A place noun takes `where` automatically, whatever the clause
       needs → *the third building where the foundation has put up*.
  M14. The relative adverb is chosen by the nearest time or place word in
       the paragraph rather than by the antecedent → `when` after *the
       reason*, because *last November* is two lines up.
  M15. `where` is only for physical places, so an abstract domain (*a
       craft*, *a field*, *a stage*) cannot take it.
  M16. `which` can fill an adverbial slot with no preposition → *the
       summer which the reservoir dried up*.
  M17. `whose` needs no following noun (M2/M3's cousin) → *the building
       whose the foundation put up*.

## 3 · The item plan

| # | keys | tests | decided by | misconceptions punished |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | `where` | abstract domain, complete clause | **meaning** (the antecedent is a craft, not a place) | M15, M16 |
| 2 | `which` | place antecedent, **object gap** — the caveat | signal (the transitive verb has no object) | M13, M17 |
| 3 | `when` | time antecedent, complete clause | signal (the antecedent is a season) | M13, M16 |
| 4 | `why` | reason antecedent, complete clause, with time words planted nearby | **meaning** (which noun the clause attaches to) | M14, M16 |

All four forms keyed once each. Item 2 is the caveat item: the lesson's
most emphatic block is *a place does not automatically take `where`*, and
this is the only item that springs it. Items 1 and 4 are decided by
meaning; item 4 in particular is unanswerable by scanning for the nearest
temporal expression, which is what M14 does.

## 4 · Context bank

1. Ceramics as a craft where beginners see progress fast.
2. A mountain observatory built by a foundation.
3. The summer a reservoir dried up and exposed a mill.
4. A ferry service suspended for eleven days.
5. *(spare)* A courtroom corridor where witnesses wait.
6. *(spare)* The winter a river froze hard enough to walk on.

## 5 · Difficulty recipe

1. The antecedent's category (place / time / reason) conflicts with a
   more salient noun elsewhere in the paragraph.
2. The gap in the clause is an object rather than a subject, so it is one
   word further from the blank.
3. The antecedent is abstract but locative in sense.

Not wanted: `that` as an option; a `where`-versus-`in which` decision;
vocabulary the learner has to look up to see which category the
antecedent belongs to.

# 4 · `Preposition + Relative Pronoun: In Which / For Whom / Of Which`

## 1 · The discrimination

Can rebuild the flat sentence under the relative clause (*she wrote a
cadenza **for** the pianist*, *ice is being lost **at** that rate*), see
which preposition it needs, and move that preposition in front of the
relative pronoun — knowing that only `whom` and `which` may follow one.

**Honest bound.** Stranding is correct English: *the pianist she wrote a
cadenza for* is not an error, and the app must not teach that it is. So
every item here is built so that stranding is not on offer — the
preposition is either printed in front of the blank (item 3) or is part
of the option itself, with no stranded preposition anywhere in the clause
(items 1, 2, 4). Inside that frame the rule is undisputed in every
register: **`who` and `that` cannot follow a preposition.** That is the
one thing this category keys on.

## 2 · Misconceptions

  M18. A preposition may be followed by `who`, since `who` is the
       "normal" form (M1's mirror image) → *for who*.
  M19. `that` follows a preposition like any other relative pronoun →
       *the rate that it is losing ice*, *of that*.
  M20. The preposition can simply be dropped, because Turkish carries it
       as a suffix inside the participle (fact 3) → *the rate which it is
       losing ice*.
  M21. The preposition is chosen by habit or by the nearest verb rather
       than by meaning → *by whom* where the sense is *for whom*.
  M22. `whose` and `the … of which` are the same string in the same
       place, so either order works → *a fund whose was never written*.
  M23. A personal pronoun can close the clause (MX3) → *the purpose of it
       was never written down*.

## 3 · The item plan

| # | keys | tests | decided by | misconceptions punished |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | `for whom` | choosing the **preposition** and the person form together | **meaning** (who wrote what for whom, stated two clauses earlier) | M18, M21 |
| 2 | `of which` | `noun + of which` word order, non-human | signal (the noun is already printed before the blank) | M22, M23 |
| 3 | `whom` | the pronoun after a **printed** preposition | signal (`with` sits immediately before the blank) | M18, M19 |
| 4 | `at which` | the preposition the antecedent itself requires | **meaning** (*losing ice at that rate*) | M19, M20 |

Two human keys, two non-human; two items where the blank carries the
preposition and one where the paragraph does. Items 1 and 4 are decided
by meaning: in item 1 three of the four options are grammatical strings
and only the sense of the passage rules out `by whom`.

## 4 · Context bank

1. A composer's dedication to a pianist she never met.
2. A neighbourhood fund set up after a fire.
3. Two teachers sharing a staff room for eleven years.
4. Disagreement over how fast a glacier is losing ice.
5. *(spare)* A dockyard crane nobody is licensed to operate.
6. *(spare)* A charity auction and the painter it was named after.

## 5 · Difficulty recipe

1. The preposition is determined by a verb that appeared in an earlier
   sentence, not next to the blank.
2. The antecedent noun collocates with a preposition (`the rate at`, `the
   extent to`) the learner has never had to produce.
3. Two options are grammatical English strings and only meaning separates
   them.

Not wanted: an item where the stranded version would also fit the blank;
a preposition so rare the item becomes vocabulary; `in which` against
`where` with a physical place, which is a free choice.

# 5 · `Reduced Relative Clauses: -ing vs -ed`

## 1 · The discrimination

Can decide whether the noun **does** the action (`-ing`) or **has it done
to it** (past participle), and knows the reduction drops the relative
pronoun **and** the auxiliary — so no finite verb may survive in the
reduced clause.

**Honest bound.** The full clause is always correct too: *the documents
that were sent* is as good as *the documents sent*, so no item may be
keyed on reduced-versus-full. Every blank here is positioned so that only
a non-finite form can fill it — the main clause already has its verb, and
a finite option would leave the sentence with two. For the same reason a
full `which/that + verb` option is never offered: it would be a second
defensible answer. This category therefore tests **voice**, not
reduction, and its lesson should say so.

## 2 · Misconceptions

  M24. The participle marks **tense**, not voice — the event is in the
       past, so it takes `-ed` → *the families waited for news*.
  M25. The auxiliary survives the reduction → *the documents were sent to
       the ministry were returned*, two finite verbs in one clause.
  M26. The reduction copies the collocation the learner has heard most
       often, not the roles in this sentence → *a company employed four
       hundred weavers* for a company that employs them.
  M27. `rise` and `raise` (and `fall`/`drop`, `lie`/`lay`) are one verb →
       an intransitive verb used where a passive participle is needed.
  M28. `to + verb` can modify a noun in place of a participle → *the
       documents to send*.

## 3 · The item plan

| # | keys | tests | decided by | misconceptions punished |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | `-ed` (`sent`) | passive reduction, clear patient | signal (the noun cannot send) | M24, M25 |
| 2 | `-ing` (`waiting`) | active reduction with the event in the past | **meaning** (past time does not make it passive) | M24, M25 |
| 3 | `-ing` (`employing`) | active reduction against a strong passive collocation — the caveat | **meaning** (the company employs; the weavers are employed) | M25, M26 |
| 4 | `-ed` (`raised`) | passive reduction with a transitivity trap | meaning + form | M25, M27 |

Two `-ing`, two `-ed`. Items 2 and 3 are the meaning-decided ones. Item 3
is the caveat item: `employed` is the form this audience has met a
hundred times and the one the sentence cannot take. Every item offers a
finite option, because M25 is the error that survives longest.

## 4 · Context bank

1. Folders and documents returned unopened by a ministry.
2. Families in a hospital corridor before dawn.
3. A textile company that built two streets of workers' housing.
4. A choir's cathedral concerts paying for a roof.
5. *(spare)* Bicycles abandoned at a railway station.
6. *(spare)* A dictionary compiled by two brothers over forty years.

## 5 · Difficulty recipe

1. The noun is a plausible patient of a different verb in the sentence,
   so both voices feel available until the roles are checked.
2. The main clause's finite verb is far from the blank, so the
   two-finite-verbs error is not audible.
3. The verb has an intransitive twin (`rise`/`raise`).

Not wanted: a full `that + verb` option (always defensible); a participle
whose `-ing` and `-ed` forms are both true of the situation; an irregular
past participle chosen to test vocabulary rather than voice.

# 6 · `Quantifier + Relative Pronoun: Some Of Which / All Of Whom`

## 1 · The discrimination

Can attach a statement about **part of a set** to the noun naming that
set with a single non-defining clause — `quantifier + of + which/whom` —
instead of starting a second sentence, and picks `whom` for people and
`which` for things after the preposition `of`.

**Honest bound.** The two-sentence version (*… nine thousand samples.
Some of them have not been tested*) is perfectly good English, so an item
can only be keyed inside a sentence the paragraph has already committed
to a comma — the `of them` option must be wrong **as a continuation of
this sentence**, which is exactly the comma splice MX3 produces. What
this category must not key on is which quantifier reads better: `many
of which` against `some of which` is a free choice, so the quantifier is
printed identically across all four options and only the pronoun varies —
except in item 3, where `neither` against `none` is a genuine numerical
difference and the paragraph counts the set.

## 2 · Misconceptions

  M29. Two clauses may be joined by a comma and a quantified pronoun
       (MX3) → *…, some of them have not been tested*.
  M30. `of` is not a real preposition here, so `who` may follow it →
       *both of who*.
  M31. `which` covers people as well as things (M6) → *all of which* for
       nine applicants.
  M32. `neither` and `none` are the same word for "hiçbiri", since
       Turkish does not count the set → `neither of which` for three
       proposals.
  M33. `whose` can follow a quantifier → *all of whose had paid*.

## 3 · The item plan

| # | keys | tests | decided by | misconceptions punished |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | `some of which` | non-human set, part of it | signal (the comma, and an inanimate antecedent) | M29, M31 |
| 2 | `both of whom` | human set of exactly two | signal (`two sisters`) | M29, M30, M31 |
| 3 | `none of which` | **how large the set is** | **meaning** (the paragraph says three) | M29, M31, M32 |
| 4 | `all of whom` | human set, whole of it | **meaning** (who signed up, and who paid) | M29, M31, M33 |

Two `of which`, two `of whom`. Item 3 is the meaning-decided one and also
the caveat item: everything else in the category is settled by animacy,
and `neither`/`none` is the one place where counting the set decides.
Every item offers the `of them` splice, because it is the error the
Turkish sentence structure produces directly and the one the lesson is
built on.

## 4 · Context bank

1. A valley seed bank with untested samples.
2. Two sisters who started a dairy after ten years abroad.
3. Three tram proposals and a fourth drawn up by a student.
4. A hiking club's cancelled winter walk and its refunds.
5. *(spare)* A bequest of paintings, half of them uncatalogued.
6. *(spare)* Six wind turbines on a ridge, two of them out of service.

## 5 · Difficulty recipe

1. The size of the set is stated in an earlier sentence, not next to the
   blank.
2. The antecedent's animacy is carried by a collective noun (*a crew*,
   *the intake*) rather than by an obvious person word.
3. The `of them` splice reads perfectly naturally to a Turkish ear, so
   the item's difficulty is entirely in noticing the comma.

Not wanted: a choice between two quantifiers that both fit; an antecedent
whose animacy is genuinely ambiguous; `of whose` as anything but a rare
fourth option.
