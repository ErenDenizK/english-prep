m3 and m4 are mirrors of one another, and that is good design, not a

key as its trap. What weakens them is only that the trap is reproduced
three times inside each item.

### Where the category label does the work the paragraph should do

The Test screen prints the category above the question, so anything the
label gives away is given away to the learner too. Nine items can be
answered with the paragraph deleted:

- **`Passive with Modals`** (p5, p6, p7, p8) — the label names the
  structure, the modal is fixed in all four options, and exactly one
  option is a well-formed passive. Nothing in "all fire exits ____ clear
  at all times" is consulted. This category tests one fact four times.
- **`Modal Perfects in Passive`** (p9, p10, p12) — the label says
  *perfect*, and each of these three offers exactly one well-formed
  perfect passive. Only **p11** (three perfect options: *must have been*,
  *can't have been*, *should have been*) makes the learner weigh the modal
  meanings the category is named for. Three quarters of the category is
  a form-recognition exercise wearing a deduction label.
- **`Causative: Have/Get Something Done`** (p13, p14, p15) — the label
  contains the answers. p13 keys *had*, p14 *got*, p15 *have*.

Two further categories are form-decidable without being label-leaks:
`Passive Reporting Structures` (p17, p18, p20 each have exactly one
well-formed option), and `Time Expressions & Signal Words`, where the
decision is made by the word immediately beside the blank and the rest of
the sentence is scenery (D4 as a category pattern, per the brief's "fine
once per category, fatal as a pattern").

### A role that survives shuffling

The brief warns off letter patterns; this is the thing that looks similar
and is real. Across the twelve items of `Passive with Modals`,
`Modal Perfects in Passive` and `Passive Reporting Structures`, **nine
keys are the only structurally well-formed option in their item**. A
test-wise student who reads no paragraph and no meaning — who simply
picks the option that is legal English — scores 9/12. That is a property
of the options, not their order, and it is what a coached student learns
first.

## Supervisor's measurement — added after the pass, by the session that ran it

The reviewer above could not compute their own agreement rate, because
they never saw a key. This section was written afterwards by comparing
every row of §1 against `*-key.json` — the keys `tools/blind-corpus.mjs`
wrote back beside the sources, outside the directory the reviewer was
pointed at.

**73 of 73. One hundred per cent agreement, zero mis-keyed items.**

That number is worth stating precisely because of what it does and does
not mean.

It means the corpus has **no wrong answers**. Every key in the three
oldest topics is the answer a competent reader reaches from the paragraph
alone. This was the open question — these three topics predate the review
pipeline and had never had a blind pass, and `data/roadmap.json` had at
one point claimed they had.

It does not mean the items are good, and the reviewer's own findings are
where the value is. A hundred per cent from a reader who also reports
that **nine items are answerable with the paragraph deleted** and that
**nine of twelve passive-structure keys are the only well-formed option**
is partly a measurement of the corpus and partly a measurement of how
little some of these items ask. Two items (`modals-t2`, `modals-t18`)
cannot discriminate at all, and eight more carry a second answer that
survives the paragraph. Those are the repair list; a wrong key would have
been a different and easier problem.

The confidence spread supports the same reading: 57 certain, 16 probable,
**no coin-flips**. Sixteen items where the reviewer could choose but was
unwilling to call a second option wrong is the honest shape of this
corpus.
