# Category spec: Quantifiers & Determiners

Six categories, written **before** any content, to the template in
`docs/agents/category-spec.md`. Taxonomy is fixed input from
`docs/agents/kickoff-v11.md` §3 — nothing here renames, adds or drops a
category.

```
Topic id:      quantifiers
Topic title:   Quantifiers & Determiners
Tier:          core-grammar
Level:         B2-C1
Questions:     4 per category (24), every one type: "cloze"
Lessons:       1 per category (6)
```

Blank 9 of the sample cloze in `docs/exam-spec.md` offered `a few / much /
plenty of / a little` — four quantifiers from three of the six categories
below, in one option list. That is the shape this topic is written for:
the options are not four forms of one word, they are four different
quantifiers, and the learner has to run **countability**, **polarity**
and **scope** over the paragraph before choosing.

## Why the misconceptions all start in the same place

Turkish does not grammaticalise countability the way English does. *Az*
covers both *few* and *little*; *çok* covers both *much* and *many*; there
is no article system standing behind *a few* against *few*, and *hiç* does
the work of both *any* (under negation) and *no*. So the recurring error
is not "picked the wrong word" — it is **not noticing that a choice was
being made**. Every misconception below is a version of that, and the
distractors are built to make the un-made choice visible.

Misconceptions are numbered **continuously across the whole topic**, M1 to
M29, so a distractor in one category can cite a misconception defined in
another — several deliberately do, because the categories share the
learner's blind spots even where they do not share forms.

# 1 · `A Few vs Few vs A Little vs Little`

## 1 · The discrimination

A learner who has this right can read a following clause — *and that was
enough to…*, *so the class was cancelled* — and decide whether the writer
is reporting **a quantity that sufficed** (`a few`, `a little`) or **a
shortfall** (`few`, `little`), and then pick the countable or uncountable
member of that pair. A learner who has it wrong hears only *az* and
chooses by the shape of the noun, or by nothing at all.

This is the strongest category in the topic and carries its hardest items:
the `a` is one character and it changes the meaning of the sentence around
it.

**Honest bound.** This category cannot discriminate *degree*. `a few` and
`several`, or `little` and `hardly any`, are not far enough apart to key an
item on, and no item may turn on how small the quantity actually was.
Nor may an item be keyed on `few` versus `little` alone with a noun whose
countability the learner cannot be expected to know (`data`, `staff`,
`fruit`): the countability half must be uncontroversial, so that the
`a`-half is what is being measured.

## 2 · Misconceptions

  M1. *Az* covers both, so the countable/uncountable line is invisible →
      "few oil", "little colonies".
  M2. *a* is only an article and does not change meaning → `few` is
      chosen while "birkaç tane var" is meant, and vice versa.
  M3. `few`/`little` read as a neutral small amount rather than as a
      shortfall → `few` in a clause that goes on to say the amount was
      enough.
  M4. Nouns countable in Turkish are assumed countable in English →
      "few evidence", "few information", "few advice".
  M5. Any small-quantity phrase can stand in for any other; the polarity
      of `hardly any` / `a bit of` is not part of the word →
      "hardly any … and that was enough".

## 3 · The item plan

| # | keys | tests | decided by | misconceptions punished |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| t1 | `a few` | countable, **sufficient** half | meaning (*more than he had dared hope … enough to rebuild*) | M2, M3, M1 |
| t2 | `few` | countable, **shortfall** half | meaning (*so the tutor cancelled the class*) | M2, M3, M1 |
| t3 | `a little` | uncountable, **sufficient** half | meaning (*that was enough to get the tractor…*) | M2, M3, M5, M1 |
| t4 | `little` | uncountable, **shortfall** half, on a noun that looks countable in Turkish | meaning + countability | M3, M4, M5 |

Both halves of both contrasts are keyed, 2:2 on the article and 2:2 on
countability, so no guessing strategy beats 25%. All four are decided by
meaning: there is no trigger word beside any of these blanks, only the
noun, and the noun never decides between `a few` and `few`. **t4 punishes
the rule**: `evidence` is what a Turkish speaker counts and English does
not, so the learner who has learned "few for things you can count" is
counting in the wrong language.

## 4 · Context bank

1. A beekeeper's hives after a storm. 2. A parish literacy course nobody
enrols on. 3. A tractor sump with oil left in it. 4. An archaeological
trench and its sponsor. 5. A soup kitchen's volunteer rota. 6. A seed
library and its returns. 7. A trawler's remaining fuel. 8. A choir short
of tenors.

Used: 1 → t1, 2 → t2, 3 → t3, 4 → t4. Spare: 5–8. No scenario is shared
by two items, and none may be reused by the lesson's own examples — the
`check` blocks are drawn from these four.

## 5 · Difficulty recipe

1. The clause that fixes sufficiency sits **after** the blank, and is
   phrased as a consequence rather than as a quantity.
2. The noun's countability is unambiguous but not obvious to a Turkish
   reader (`evidence`, `progress`).
3. The paragraph's overall tone runs against the key — a disaster opening
   with a `a few` key (t1), a full postal drop with a `few` key (t2).

Not wanted: rare vocabulary in the paragraph, a quantity so small the item
becomes arithmetic, or a context where a careful reader could argue either
polarity.

|---|---|---|---|
| `a few` | t1 | t2 | |
| `few` | t2 | t1, t3, t4 | |
| `a little` | t3 | t1, t4 | |
| `little` | t4 | t1, t2, t3 | |

If the lesson teaches `several` or `a couple of`, they become holes: no

at all.

# 2 · `Much vs Many vs A Lot Of vs Plenty Of`

## 1 · The discrimination

A learner who has this right can pick a quantifier by two facts about its
**environment** — is the noun countable, and is the clause affirmative,
negative, interrogative or inside a degree frame (`as … as`, `too`, `so`,
`how`) — rather than by how much of the stuff there is.

**Honest bound, stated in the kickoff and binding here.** `a lot of` and
`plenty of` overlap heavily in affirmative sentences, and `much` and `a
lot of` overlap in negatives and questions. **No item in this category may
place `a lot of` and `plenty of` in the same option list in an affirmative
clause, and no item may place `much` and `a lot of` in the same option
list in a negative or a question.** Where they meet, both are right and
the item is broken; §7 records the three items rejected for exactly this.
Key on countability, on degree frames that `a lot of`/`plenty of` cannot
enter, and on the affirmative declarative where bare `much` is not used.

## 2 · Misconceptions

  M6. One Turkish word (*çok*) for both, so `much`/`many` are chosen by
      ear → "many traffic", "much crossings".
  M7. `a lot of` is a universal substitute for `much`/`many` → "as a lot
      of crossings as", "too a lot of noise".
  M8. `much` is simply the formal word for *çok* and works anywhere →
      "there is much time", "still gets much traffic".
  M9. `plenty of` is an exact synonym of `a lot of`, with no sufficiency
      meaning and no restriction on negatives → "no longer receives
      plenty of silt".
  M10. Mass nouns that take a plural in Turkish are treated as countable
      → "many traffics", "many silt", "a few luggage".

## 3 · The item plan

| # | keys | tests | decided by | misconceptions punished |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| t5 | `much` | uncountable, **negative** environment | signal (polarity + noun) | M6, M9, M10 |
| t6 | `many` | countable, **degree frame** `as … as` | signal (frame + noun) | M6, M7, M9 |
| t7 | `plenty of` | uncountable, **affirmative**, sufficiency | meaning (*don't rush … before the exhibition opens*) | M8, M3, M6 |
| t8 | `a lot of` | uncountable, **affirmative** | meaning + environment | M8, M10, M6 |

Both halves of the countability contrast are keyed (t5, t7, t8
uncountable; t6 countable) and both halves of the environment contrast are
keyed (t5, t6 non-affirmative; t7, t8 affirmative). **t7 and t8 are
decided by meaning and register together, not by a trigger** — the noun
alone leaves `much`, `a lot of` and `plenty of` all standing, and it is
the affirmative declarative that removes `much`. **t8 punishes the rule**:
the learner who has been taught "uncountable → much" meets an uncountable
noun where `much` is exactly wrong.

## 4 · Context bank

1. A dammed river and a starved marsh. 2. A winter ferry timetable.
3. Glue drying on a picture frame. 4. A bypass that did not empty the
market square. 5. A printworks and its paper stock. 6. A campsite's
water pressure in August. 7. An orchestra's rehearsal hours. 8. A tip
shop and its donated books.

Used: 1 → t5, 2 → t6, 3 → t7, 4 → t8. Spare: 5–8.

## 5 · Difficulty recipe

1. The environment that decides (negation, `as … as`) is separated from
   the blank by the noun phrase, so the learner has to parse the clause.
2. The noun is a mass noun that Turkish pluralises (`traffic`, `silt`).
3. Two of the four options are eliminated by countability and the
   remaining pair by environment — never by preference.

Not wanted: a choice between `a lot of` and `plenty of`; a choice between
`much` and `a lot of` under negation; an option ruled out only because it
is informal.

## 6 · Coverage ledger

| form | key | distractor | absent |
|---|---|---|---|
| `much` | t5 | t6, t7, t8 | |
| `many` | t6 | t5, t8 | |
| `plenty of` | t7 | t5, t6 | |
| `a lot of` | t8 | t6 | |
| `a few` | | t5, t8 | |
| `few`, `little` | | t7 | |
| `a great deal of`, `a large number of`, `lots of` | | | **absent** |

The last row is the hole to watch: if the lesson teaches `a great deal of`
or `a large number of` as the formal register partners, no question ever
offers them, and the learner can score 4/4 without holding them. Either
the lesson mentions them only in passing, or the supervisor swaps one into
t5's option list — where `a great deal of` is wrong under negation for the
same reason `plenty of` is.

# 3 · `Some vs Any vs No vs None`

## 1 · The discrimination

A learner who has this right can tell **where the negation lives** — in
the verb (`did not give any reason`), in the determiner (`gave no
reason`), or in a word that is negative without looking it (`hardly`,
`without`) — and can tell a determiner (`no`, before a noun) from a
pronoun (`none`, standing alone or with `of`).

**Honest bound.** `some` and `any` genuinely overlap in questions:
*Would you like some help?* and *Would you like any help?* are both
English, and offers and requests are the classic place where the
"any-in-questions" rule fails. **No item here may be keyed on `some`
versus `any` inside a question.** Nor may an item be keyed on the
affirmative *whichever* sense of `any` (*any train will do*) against
`every`, which is often a genuine paraphrase of it.

## 2 · Misconceptions

  M11. Turkish negates the verb *and* uses *hiç*, so English gets two
       negatives too → "hardly no snow", "did not give no reason".
  M12. `any` belongs only to clauses containing `not`; implicit negatives
       (`hardly`, `scarcely`, `without`, `rarely`) are not recognised as
       negative environments.
  M13. `no` and `none` are two spellings of one word → "none bridge",
       "no is still working".
  M14. The choice among `some`/`any`/`no` is felt to be free, and is made
       without checking what the paragraph reports as fact.
  M15. Scope words (`every`, `all`) are read as emphatic versions of
       `some` → "gave every reason" where one reason was withheld.

## 3 · The item plan

| # | keys | tests | decided by | misconceptions punished |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| t9 | `any` | implicit negative (`hardly`) | signal, but one the rule gets wrong | M11, M12, M14, M6 |
| t10 | `some` | affirmative statement of fact | meaning (*so she strapped the joint*) | M14, M12, M13 |
| t11 | `no` | negation carried by the determiner, verb affirmative | meaning + structure | M11, M14, M15 |
| t12 | `none` | pronoun, no following noun | structure + scope | M13, M14, M24 |

Both halves are keyed twice: positive (`some`) against negative (`any`,
`no`, `none`), and determiner (`some`, `any`, `no`) against pronoun
(`none`). **t10 and t11 are decided by meaning** — nothing beside the
blank says which is right; the vet's strapping the joint and the
architects' guessing do. **t9 punishes the rule**: the paragraph contains
no `not`, and the rule "any needs a negative" is right for the wrong
reason unless the learner sees that `hardly` is one.

## 4 · Context bank

1. A north face with no snow left on it. 2. A vet and a dog's hock.
3. A committee's three-line refusal. 4. A village borehole scheme.
5. A night bus with an unlit stop. 6. A cellar with a smell nobody can
place. 7. A lost-property office. 8. A greenhouse after a hailstorm.

Used: 1 → t9, 2 → t10, 3 → t11, 4 → t12. Spare: 5–8.

## 5 · Difficulty recipe

1. The clause is affirmative in form and negative in meaning, or the
   reverse (t9, t11).
2. The evidence that fixes the fact comes after the blank as a
   consequence (*so she strapped the joint*).
3. The blank sits where a determiner and a pronoun would look equally
   plausible until the following word is read.

Not wanted: `some` against `any` in a question; `any` in its *whichever*
sense against `every`; an option that is wrong only because it is rare.

## 6 · Coverage ledger

| form | key | distractor | absent |
|---|---|---|---|
| `some` | t10 | t9, t11 | |
| `any` | t9 | t10, t11, t12 | |
| `no` | t11 | t9, t10, t12 | |
| `none` | t12 | t10 | |
| `every` | | t11 | |
| `neither` | | t12 | |
| `much` | | t9 | |
| `anything/nothing/no one`, `not … any` as a full clause | | | **absent** |

If the lesson teaches the pronoun series (`nothing`, `no one`, `anybody`)
it teaches something no option list offers. Recommended: the lesson names
them once as the same rule in another shape, and spends its emphasis on
`no` versus `none`, which two items exercise.

# 4 · `Each vs Every vs Both vs All`

## 1 · The discrimination

A learner who has this right can check three things before choosing: **how
many** are in the set (two, or more than two), whether the noun is
**singular, plural or uncountable**, and whether the verb agrees. A
learner who has it wrong maps *her* onto `each`/`every` at random and
never looks at the verb.

**Honest bound, and it is the tight one in this topic.** `each` and
`every` are interchangeable in most sentences a B2 learner will meet:
*each student was given a badge* and *every student was given a badge*
report the same fact, and an item keyed there is unanswerable. They may be
keyed apart **only** where the grammar forces it: after `almost / nearly /
practically` (only `every`), before `of` (only `each`, or `every one of`),
with a plural subject and a floating quantifier (*they each*), and where
the set is exactly two (only `each`). Likewise `both` and `all` may not be
keyed against each other over a set of two, where `all` is merely marked
rather than wrong.

## 2 · Misconceptions

  M16. *Her* covers both, so `each` and `every` are freely swapped →
       "practically each flat".
  M17. Determiner–verb agreement is not part of the choice → "each were",
       "both of them carries".
  M18. Scope is not checked: `all` is used with a singular noun, `both`
       with a set larger than two.
  M19. Structural restrictions are invisible → "every of them", "each the
       plasterwork", "all flat".
  M20. `both` and `neither` are chosen from the number *two* alone, without
       checking what the paragraph says actually happened.

## 3 · The item plan

| # | keys | tests | decided by | misconceptions punished |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| t13 | `both` | set of exactly two, plural verb | meaning (*had to drag them up the stairs*) + agreement | M17, M18, M20 |
| t14 | `every` | `practically` + singular noun, large set | signal (the modifier) | M16, M18 |
| t15 | `each` | `of` + pronoun, singular verb, set of two, distributive | structure + meaning | M19, M17, M22 |
| t16 | `all` | uncountable noun, totality | meaning (*not a patch of it could be saved*) | M19, M10, M29 |


Keys spread across all four forms, across singular/plural/uncountable
nouns, and across set sizes two and many. **t13 and t16 are decided by
meaning** — `neither` in t13 and `most of` in t16 are both grammatical and
are eliminated only by the paragraph's last clause. **t15 punishes the
rule**: it is the one place where `each` and `every` genuinely come apart
(`every` never takes `of`), and it is also where the set of two makes
`each` right and `every` wrong for a second, independent reason.

## 4 · Context bank

1. A station with two lifts in a heatwave. 2. A flooded block of flats and
one insurance assessor. 3. A treaty signed in two capitals. 4. A damp
chapel taken on by a trust. 5. A ringed pair of ospreys. 6. A vineyard's
two presses. 7. A block's recycling bins. 8. A rescue boat's crew list.

Used: 1 → t13, 2 → t14, 3 → t15, 4 → t16. Spare: 5–8.

## 5 · Difficulty recipe

1. The set size is stated once, early, and not repeated beside the blank.
2. The verb form beside the blank quietly eliminates two options, and the
   paragraph's outcome eliminates the third.
3. The noun is uncountable, so the countable quantifiers fail on a ground
   the learner is not looking at (t16).

Not wanted: `each` against `every` in a neutral frame; `both` against
`all` over two things; an option ruled out only by register.

## 6 · Coverage ledger

| form | key | distractor | absent |
|---|---|---|---|
| `each` | t15 | t13, t14, t16 | |
| `every` | t14 | t13, t15, t16 | |
| `both` | t13 | t14, t15 | |
| `all` | t16 | t14 | |
| `neither` | | t13 | |
| `either` | | t15 | |
| `most of` | | t16 | |
| `every one of`, `each other`, `all of the` as a distinct form, `whole` | | | **absent** |

`every one of` is the form the lesson must teach as the repair for
"every of them", and no option list offers it. That is acceptable only
because t15 keys the alternative it repairs; if the lesson gives it a
`forms` block of its own, ask the supervisor to swap it into t15's list in
place of `both`.

# 5 · `Either vs Neither vs Nor`

## 1 · The discrimination

A learner who has this right can count the negatives in a clause before
adding one. Given a set of exactly two, they can tell whether the negation
is already in the verb (`did not accept either`) or has to be supplied by
the determiner (`neither bridge has been repaired`), and they can complete
a correlative pair without crossing it (`either … or`, `neither … nor`).

**Honest bound.** Clause-initial `neither` and clause-initial `nor` are
interchangeable as inverted linkers: *and neither has the treasurer* and
*nor has the treasurer* are both standard. **No item may be keyed on
`nor` against `neither` in that position.** `nor` is keyable only as the
second element of a `neither … nor` pair, where nothing else can stand.
Nor may an item be keyed on `either` versus `each` in phrases like *on
either side* / *on each side*, which mean the same thing.

## 2 · Misconceptions

  M21. Turkish *ne … ne de* keeps the verb negative, so English keeps its
       negative verb too → "did not accept neither tender".
  M22. `either` is read as *ikisi de* rather than *ikisinden biri*, and
       `neither` as a general *hiçbiri* usable for any set size.
  M23. Correlative pairs are crossed → "neither … or", "both … or".
  M24. The two-versus-more-than-two line is not drawn between `neither`
       and `none` → "none of the two".

## 3 · The item plan

| # | keys | tests | decided by | misconceptions punished |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| t17 | `neither` | determiner, set of two, verb affirmative | meaning (*the bus takes a twelve-mile detour*) | M22, M13, M17 |
| t18 | `nor` | second element of `neither … nor` | signal (the pair) | M23 |
| t19 | `either` | verb already negative | structure + meaning | M21, M17, M16 |
| t20 | `neither` | pronoun with `of`, set of two, singular verb | meaning (*has asked for another pass*) | M24, M22, M16 |

`neither` is keyed twice on purpose — once as a determiner, once as a
pronoun — because they are different forms with different agreement, and
because `nor` is only keyable in one position (see the honest bound).
**t17 and t20 are decided by meaning**: `either` and `each` are
grammatical in both and are eliminated only by the consequence the
paragraph reports. **t19 punishes the rule**: the learner who has just
learned "two things, negative → neither" produces the double negative that
Turkish licenses and English does not.

## 4 · Context bank

1. Two bridges damaged by ice. 2. A car park machine that takes no cash.
3. Two tenders for a roof. 4. A probe's two sets of crater images.
5. Two candidates for a harbour-master's post. 6. A pair of tide gauges.
7. Two translations of a poem. 8. A hostel with two boilers.

Used: 1 → t17, 2 → t18, 3 → t19, 4 → t20. Spare: 5–8. Scenarios 3 and 5
are deliberately not both used: they are the same shape (a body rejects
two candidates) even though the nouns differ, and a shared shape is a
shared scenario for review purposes.

## 5 · Difficulty recipe

1. The verb's polarity is fixed several words before the blank, so the
   learner has to hold it (t19).
2. The set size is stated as a number, not as a pair-word, so `none`
   stays superficially plausible (t20).
3. The consequence that decides between the positive and negative reading
   arrives in the final clause.

Not wanted: `nor` against `neither` as an inverted linker; `either` against
`each` in *on either side*; an option that is wrong only because it is
formal.

## 6 · Coverage ledger

| form | key | distractor | absent |
|---|---|---|---|
| `neither` (determiner) | t17 | t18, t19 | |
| `neither of` (pronoun) | t20 | | |
| `either` (determiner, negative clause) | t19 | t17, t18 | |
| `either of` | | t20 | |

Sentence-final `either` (*and the north gully is not much better either*)
cannot be tested here without putting `too`/`also` into an option list,
and they are adverbs, not determiners. The inverted `neither/nor do I`
pattern is unkeyable per the honest bound. Both belong in the lesson as
recognition-only material, flagged as such.

# 6 · `Most vs Most Of vs The Most`

## 1 · The discrimination

A learner who has this right can look at the **word after the blank** and
know which form the slot takes — a bare noun takes `most`, a determiner or
pronoun (`the`, `its`, `them`) takes `most of`, a superlative comparison
takes `the most` — and can tell the two meanings apart: `most X` is *the
majority of X*, `the most X` is *more X than anyone else*.

**Honest bound.** `most` and `most of the` are both correct in sentences
where the noun phrase is generic in one reading and specific in the other
(*most students* / *most of the students* on this course), so an item may
not be keyed on that choice unless the following word settles it. And
`the most` may not be keyed against a comparative in a two-way comparison,
which is a comparatives item and belongs to `closest-meaning`.

## 2 · Misconceptions

  M25. *-in çoğu* is carried across whole → "most of students", "most of
       people".
  M26. `most` and `most of` are felt to be the same word with an optional
       tail → "most the animals", "most its funding".
  M27. `the most` is read as an emphatic *çoğu* rather than as a
       superlative → `the most` where a proportion is meant.
  M28. The superlative's article is droppable, or `more` can serve for a
       set of three or more → "most complaints of any ward", "more
       complaints of any ward".
  M29. Proportion words are chosen without checking the figure in the
       paragraph: *most*, *almost all*, *all* and *none of* are treated as
       interchangeable degrees of "a lot".

## 3 · The item plan

| # | keys | tests | decided by | misconceptions punished |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| t21 | `most` | bare plural noun, generalisation | structure + meaning (*fifty-eight per cent*) | M25, M27, M29 |
| t22 | `most of` | before a possessive determiner | structure + meaning (*and the rest*) | M26, M27, M29 |
| t23 | `the most` | superlative against a proportion | **meaning** (*the other three still received the majority*) | M27, M28, M25 |
| t24 | `most of` | before `the` + plural noun | structure + meaning (*the ones that were not*) | M26, M27, M29 |

Both halves are keyed: the bare form once, the `of` form twice (before a
possessive and before `the`, which are different slots the learner fails
differently), and the superlative once. **t23 is decided by meaning
alone** — `most complaints` and `the most complaints` are both grammatical
in the slot and only the final clause separates the majority reading from
the superlative one. **t23 also punishes the rule**: everything the
learner has been taught in t21, t22 and t24 says the deciding factor is
the word after the blank, and here it is not.

## 4 · Context bank

1. An airline's safety-card survey. 2. A community centre funded by one
trust. 3. A hospital trust's complaints, by ward. 4. A hedgehog rescue
centre's autumn intake. 5. A canal towpath's cyclists. 6. A seed bank's
germination rates. 7. A market's stallholders. 8. A river's summer
readings.

Used: 1 → t21, 2 → t22, 3 → t23, 4 → t24. Spare: 5–8.

## 5 · Difficulty recipe

1. A real figure in the paragraph makes one proportion word true and the
   others false (t21's 58%, t23's 31 of 90).
2. The word after the blank is a possessive rather than `the`, which is
   the slot learners have met least (t22).
3. The superlative reading and the majority reading are both available in
   the sentence, and only a later clause rules one out (t23).

Not wanted: `most` against `most of the` where both are correct; a
superlative in a two-way comparison; an option that is wrong only because
it is wordy.

`most of them` is the pronoun slot and appears nowhere; it is one word
away from t22's `most of its`, so the lesson may teach it as the same
rule. `mostly` is an adverb and must not appear in an option list — if the
lesson teaches it, it teaches something the test cannot reach, which is
fine only if the lesson says so.

# 7 · Rejected keyings

Kept because §1's honest bounds are cheap to write and easy to forget by
item three. Each of these was drafted as a paragraph and thrown away
because two options could both be reported as the same fact.

1. **`much` against `a lot of` under negation** (cat. 2). *The marsh no
   longer receives ____ silt* — both are correct English. Fixed by
   removing `a lot of` from t5's option list rather than by rewriting the
   paragraph, because no paragraph fixes it.
2. **`plenty of` against `a lot of` in the affirmative** (cat. 2). *There
   is ____ time before the exhibition opens* — both correct. `a lot of`
   was removed from t7 and `plenty of` from t8; the two are never offered
   together in an affirmative clause anywhere in the topic.
3. **`many` against `a lot of` in a negative** (cat. 2). *The archive has
   not digitised ____ of its early photographs* — both correct. Replaced
   by the `as … as` frame in t6, which `a lot of` cannot enter.
4. **`each` against `every` in a plain exhaustive statement** (cat. 4).
   *The inspector insisted that ____ extinguisher be checked* — both
   correct, and the "not just the ones on the ground floor" clause does
   not separate them. Replaced by `practically every` (t14).
5. **`both` against `each` with a set of two** (cat. 4). *The station has
   two lifts and ____ of them failed* — `each of them failed` and `both of
   them failed` report the same fact. Fixed by moving to a plural verb, so
   agreement decides, and by keeping `each` as an option only where the
   verb is singular (t15).
6. **`each` against `both` where the verb was left flexible** (cat. 4,
   t15). *`both of them carry a different date`* is acceptable; the item
   survives only because the verb printed in the paragraph is `carries`.
   A reviewer changing that verb breaks the item.
7. **`nor` against `neither` as a clause linker** (cat. 5). *The insurers
   refused, ____ would the council accept responsibility* — both
   standard. Replaced by the second slot of `neither … nor` (t18).
8. **`either` against `each` in *on either side*** (cat. 5). *There are
   ditches on ____ side* — `either side`, `each side` and (with a plural
   noun) `both sides` all report the same fact. Abandoned entirely.
9. **`neither` against `none` with an unstated set size** (cat. 5). *and
   ____ of them was prepared to move* — `none of them` is defensible in
   speech when the set is two. Fixed by writing the number into the
   paragraph, so t20 reads `of the two`, where `none of the two` is not
   English.
10. **`some` against `any` in an offer** (cat. 3). *Would you like ____
    help with the boxes* — both correct. The whole question environment is
    barred by §1's honest bound for that category.
11. **`any` against `every` in the *whichever* sense** (cat. 3). *The pass
    is valid on ____ train that stops at the branch line* — `any train`
    and `every train` are both true of the same timetable. Abandoned;
    `every` survives only as a scope distractor in t11.
12. **`most` against `the most` where the majority and the maximum
    coincide** (cat. 6). Any paragraph in which one ward has more than
    half of the complaints makes both options true. Fixed by the
    arithmetic in t23: 31 of 90 is the largest share and not a majority.
