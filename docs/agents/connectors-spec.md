`docs/agents/category-spec.md`. Taxonomy is fixed input from

adds or drops a category.

## What this topic is, and the one design rule that generates the rest

The sample paper's own discourse-marker blank offered `Similarly /
Nevertheless / In spite of / Since` — four markers of four **different
logical functions**. Nothing in that item can be answered by ranking
near-synonyms; it is answered by reading the relation between two
sentences and naming it.

So the whole topic is built on one rule:

> **The paragraph must establish the relation. The options must name four
> different relations.** If the paragraph does not make the relation
> decidable, the item is a vocabulary guess and the four options are four
> words the learner half-recognises.

Two consequences, both binding on every item below.

**A · Two markers of the same function never share an option list unless
the paragraph rules one out for a reason a teacher can name.** The
nameable reasons used in this topic are exactly four, and none of them is
register:

| Reason | Example in this topic |
| --- | --- |
| **Word class** — an adverb cannot join two clauses after a comma | t8: `whereas` against `however / nevertheless / on the other hand` |
| **What follows the blank** — a clause with its own subject, or a bare infinitive | t10 / t11: `so that` against `in order to` |
| **Cardinality of what is being pointed at** — one thing, or one of many | t13 / t14: `namely` against `for instance` |
| **Whether the subject changes** — new point about the same subject, or the same behaviour in a second subject | t1–t4: `moreover` against `similarly / likewise` |

**B · Register differences are never keyed.** `moreover`/`furthermore`/`in
addition`, `therefore`/`thus`/`consequently`/`hence`,
`similarly`/`likewise` in most frames, `nevertheless`/`nonetheless`,
`in short`/`to sum up`, `meanwhile`/`in the meantime`,
`subsequently`/`afterwards`, `for instance`/`for example` are treated as
interchangeable throughout. Where the kickoff's category name puts two
same-function markers side by side, one of them is keyed and the other is
a distractor **in the other function's items** — never a distractor
against its own twin.

### Distractor discipline

Every distractor in all 24 items cites a numbered misconception below.
The topic-wide ones, which recur across categories:

- **M0. A discourse marker is a decoration you sprinkle on a paragraph.**
  → any marker whose function is not in the passage at all. This is the
  misconception the whole topic exists to break, and it is why an option
  list mixes functions rather than synonyms.
- **M0b. English connectives behave like Turkish ones and can join two
  clauses with a comma.** → the comma splice (`…, however …`).
  Turkish `ama / oysa / ancak` are conjunctions; their nearest English
  translations mostly are not.

# 1 · `Addition vs Similarity: Moreover / Similarly / Likewise`

## 1 · The discrimination

A learner who has this right can tell **a further, different point about
the same subject** (addition) from **a second subject behaving the same
way** (similarity) — and therefore knows that `similarly` needs two
comparable things in the passage and `moreover` does not.

**The honest bound.** This category cannot discriminate `moreover` from
`furthermore`, `in addition`, `besides` or `what is more`, nor
`similarly` from `likewise` or `in the same way`. Those are register and
rhythm. No item may put two of them in one option list, and no
explanation may argue that one is "more natural".

## 2 · Misconceptions

- **M1.** Turkish `ayrıca` covers *moreover / furthermore / in addition /
  besides / also*, so `moreover` is used as a general "and" wherever a
  second sentence appears → `Moreover` chosen for a parallel case, an
  example, or a contrast.
- **M2.** A second sentence on the same topic is "similar", so
  `similarly` is read as "and another thing" → `Similarly` chosen where
  the subject has not changed.
- **M3.** A second case is thought to be an *example* of the first
  (`örneğin` over-applied) → `For instance` chosen where two peers are
  matched.
- **M4.** Any second sentence is assumed to be "on the other hand"
  material, because Turkish `diğer taraftan` is a common filler →
  `On the other hand` chosen where both sentences point the same way.

## 3 · The item plan

| # | keys | tests | decided by | misconceptions punished |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| t1 | `Moreover` | addition | meaning — subject unchanged (`it` = the same crane), second advantage | M2, M0 (`Even so`, `By then`) |
| t2 | `Similarly` | similarity | meaning — subject changes, and "the same margin" marks the parallel | M1, M0 |
| t3 | `Likewise` | similarity | meaning — "both operators" makes two peers explicit | M3, M1, M0 |
| t4 | `Moreover` | addition — **the caveat item** | meaning — two facts *look* parallel (opening hours, stock) but share one subject | M2, M4, M0 |

Both halves keyed 2:2. No item is decided by a word sitting next to the
blank: in every one the deciding evidence is the subject of the second
sentence, which the learner has to read past the blank to find.

## 4 · Context bank

1. A container crane on a port quay. *(t1)*
2. Olive harvest dates against inland vineyards. *(t2)*
3. Island ferries and a cable car in high wind. *(t3)*
4. A night pharmacy and a hospital's late discharges. *(t4)*
5. A university library and the municipal one extending loan periods.
6. Two amateur football clubs sharing a floodlit pitch.
7. A dairy and a cheese co-operative switching to glass bottles.
8. A translated novel and its Greek edition.

## 5 · Difficulty recipe

1. The second sentence's subject is a pronoun (`it`), so the learner must
   resolve it before the relation is visible.
2. The two facts belong to the same semantic field (speed and fuel; hours
   and stock), which makes them *feel* parallel when they are not.
3. The parallel is carried by a quantity phrase ("by almost exactly the
   same margin") rather than by a marker word.

Not wanted: rare vocabulary, a marker nobody would consider, or a slot
where `moreover` and `furthermore` are both offered.

# 2 · `Contrast vs Concession: However / Nevertheless / On the Other Hand`

## 1 · The discrimination

A learner who has this right can tell **a denied expectation** (the first
sentence predicts one outcome, the second reports the opposite happening
anyway → concession) from **two sides of one thing weighed against each
other** (→ contrast), and knows that the English markers for both are
**adverbs**, not conjunctions.

**The honest bound — the hardest one in this topic.** `However` is a
general-purpose contrastive and is acceptable in most slots where
`nevertheless` or `on the other hand` fit. So **no item may be keyed on
`however` against another contrastive marker.** `However` may be keyed
only where it is the *only* contrastive option in the list, and it may be
a distractor only where the relation is not contrastive at all, or where
the syntax rules it out. Likewise `nevertheless` and `nonetheless` are
twins and never appear together.

## 2 · Misconceptions

- **M5.** Turkish `ama / fakat / ancak / oysa` are conjunctions, so
  `however` is used to join two clauses after a comma → the comma splice
  (this is M0b, and t8 is the item that springs it).
- **M6.** `On the other hand` is a general "but" (Turkish `diğer
  taraftan`) → chosen where nothing is being weighed and a decision has
  simply been taken despite an obstacle.
- **M7.** `Nevertheless` is read as "in addition to that" or as a neutral
  sentence-opener → chosen where the second sentence adds a drawback
  rather than defying an expectation.
- **M8.** A contrast is mistaken for elaboration or summary, because the
  learner reads the *topic* and not the *direction* → `For instance` /
  `In short` chosen for a sentence that contradicts the one before it.
- **M9.** Obstacle-then-action is read as cause-then-effect (Turkish
  `bu yüzden` reflex) → `Therefore` chosen where the first sentence is an
  obstacle, not a cause.

## 3 · The item plan

| # | keys | tests | decided by | misconceptions punished |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| t5 | `Nevertheless` | concession — action taken despite two obstacles | meaning | M6, M9, M0 |
| t6 | `On the other hand` | contrast — one option's plus, then its minus | meaning | M7, M0 |
| t7 | `However` | concession-flavoured reversal; **only contrastive option present**, per the bound | meaning | M8 |
| t8 | `whereas` | **the caveat item** — word class, not function: four contrastive options, one comma | signal — the punctuation | M5 / M0b |

Both halves keyed: concession t5, t7; contrast t6, t8. t7 is deliberately
*not* keyed against another contrastive marker — that is the bound being
respected rather than worked around.

## 4 · Context bank

1. A walking party, sleet and two colds. *(t5)*
2. A studio above a fish market. *(t6)*
3. A coastal path a guidebook calls easy. *(t7)*
4. Northern and southern metro lines. *(t8)*
5. A restaurant with a two-month waiting list and no parking.
6. A cheap flight that lands ninety kilometres from the city.
7. A second-hand piano that is in tune but will not fit up the stairs.
8. A grant that covers materials but not wages.

## 5 · Difficulty recipe

1. The obstacle and the action are in different sentences, so the denied
   expectation has to be reconstructed rather than read off.
2. The passage offers a plausible causal reading as well as a contrastive
   one (t5: bad weather → and yet they went).
3. All four options are contrastive and the decision is syntactic (t8) —
   which is the only item in the category where the marker's *grammar*
   decides, and the one the lesson's most emphatic block must prepare.

Not wanted: keying `however` against `nevertheless`; any explanation
whose argument is that the key "sounds better".

# 3 · `Result vs Purpose: Therefore / Thus / So That / In Order To`

## 1 · The discrimination

A learner who has this right can tell **what followed** from **what was
intended**, and can choose the purpose marker that fits what comes after
the blank: `so that` + a clause with its own subject, `in order to` + a
bare infinitive.

**The honest bound.** `therefore`, `thus`, `consequently`, `hence` and
`as a result` are one form for this purpose, and so are `so that` and
`in order that`, and `in order to` and `so as to`. **Never key one
against another of its own group.** What may be keyed: result against
purpose, purpose against cause, and which purpose marker the following
syntax allows.

## 2 · Misconceptions

- **M10.** Turkish `için` covers *for, because of, in order to* and *so
  that*, so cause and purpose collapse into one → `because` / `because
  of` chosen for a purpose clause, and the reverse.
- **M11.** `-mek için` is translated word for word as `for + verb` →
  `for have the trays cooling`. This is the single most productive
  written error this audience makes, and it is a live option, not a dead
  one.
- **M12.** Turkish `böylece` covers both *thus* (result) and *so that*
  (purpose) → `so that` chosen for an unintended consequence, which is
  the trap t12 is built on.
- **M13.** `so that` and `in order to` are believed interchangeable, the
  following syntax being invisible → `in order to + subject`, or
  `so that + bare verb`.
- **M14.** A result marker is thought to be a conjunction that can join
  clauses after a comma → `thus` / `therefore` in a slot that needs one
  (M0b again).

## 3 · The item plan

| # | keys | tests | decided by | misconceptions punished |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| t9 | `therefore` | result, mid-clause position | meaning — the relation is a consequence, and the marker sits after the auxiliary | M0, M9 |
| t10 | `so that` | purpose + clause | signal — a subject follows the blank | M13, M10, M12/M14 (`thus`) |
| t11 | `in order to` | purpose + bare infinitive | signal — a bare verb follows the blank | M13, M11, M10 |
| t12 | `so` | result — **the caveat item**: an *unintended* consequence, where `böylece` pulls the learner to purpose | meaning | M12, M10, M0 |

Both halves keyed 2:2 (result t9, t12; purpose t10, t11). t9 and t12 are
decided by meaning; t10 and t11 by what follows the blank — which is the
one signal this category is allowed to key on, because it is a rule and
not a preference.

## 4 · Context bank

1. A marina flood barrier after a high tide. *(t9)*
2. A village school moving lessons for the bus. *(t10)*
3. A bakery's mixing time and the market porters. *(t11)*
4. Withdrawn buses on a cannery route. *(t12)*
5. A vet's clinic keeping a Saturday slot free for emergencies.
6. A hillside terraced to stop the topsoil washing down.
7. A choir rehearsing in the church because of the acoustics.
8. A pharmacy's fridge alarm wired to the owner's phone.

## 5 · Difficulty recipe

1. The consequence is plausible *as* an intention until the reader
   notices nobody would have wanted it (t12).
2. The deciding evidence is what comes **after** the blank, so the
   learner cannot stop reading at the blank (t10, t11).
3. The marker sits mid-clause (`has ____ asked`), where Turkish would put
   `bu yüzden` at the front.

Not wanted: `therefore` against `thus`; `so that` against `in order
that`; a purpose item whose other three options cannot even be
pronounced in the slot.

## 6 · Coverage ledger

|---|---|---|---|

conjunction). Teach it as `therefore`'s twin and say so.

# 4 · `Exemplification vs Restatement: For Instance / Namely / In Other Words`

## 1 · The discrimination

A learner who has this right can tell three moves apart: giving **one
member of a set** (`for instance`), giving **the complete identity of
something already referred to** (`namely`), and **saying the same thing
again in different words** (`in other words`). The test is cardinality
and novelty: many → one, one → itself, and same content → new wording.

**The honest bound.** `for instance` and `for example` are one form;
`namely`, `that is` and `i.e.` are near enough that a competent teacher
would accept any of them in most slots; `in other words` and `to put it
another way` likewise. No item offers two members of one of those groups
as rivals. `such as` is a preposition and belongs to a different lesson;
it is kept out of every option list here rather than keyed against
`for instance`.

## 2 · Misconceptions

- **M15.** Turkish `yani` covers *namely, that is, in other words* and
  *I mean*, so all three English markers feel like one word → whichever
  is offered gets chosen.
- **M16.** `Namely` is read as "for example" → chosen where the passage
  has established a set of many.
- **M17.** `In other words` is read as "and so" or "in conclusion" →
  chosen where the second sentence adds new content rather than rewording
  the old.
- **M18.** New specific detail is assumed to be a *restatement* because
  it is about the same thing → the mirror image of M16, and what t16
  punishes.
- **M19.** A contrast or a summary marker is chosen because the second
  sentence is simply *shorter* or *later* → `by contrast`, `in short`.

## 3 · The item plan

| # | keys | tests | decided by | misconceptions punished |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| t13 | `For instance` | exemplification | meaning — "almost every object" establishes many | M16, M15, M17 |
| t14 | `namely` | specification | meaning — "only one of the four bidders" forbids many | M15, M16 (reversed), M19 |
| t15 | `In other words` | restatement | meaning — the second sentence is the first in plain language, with no new fact | M17, M15, M0 |
| t16 | `for instance` | exemplification — **the caveat item**, marker in parenthetical mid-sentence position | meaning — same object, but the content is new and specific | M18, M19 |

Both halves keyed 2:2 (exemplification t13, t16; restatement/
specification t14, t15). All four are decided by meaning; the category
has no trigger word to key on, which is exactly why the cardinality test
has to be stated in the lesson.

## 4 · Context bank

1. A museum conservation team inventing mounts. *(t13)*
2. A tender with one qualifying bidder. *(t14)*
3. A swimming pool closed "for an indeterminate duration". *(t15)*
4. Pre-war ships and their steam steering gear. *(t16)*
5. A seed bank and the varieties it keeps.
6. A council's parking exemptions.
7. A biscuit factory's allergen labelling.
8. A theatre's rule about latecomers.

## 5 · Difficulty recipe

1. The number that decides ("almost every object", "only one of the
   four") sits in the sentence *before* the blank and is easy to skim
   past.
2. The example is a long noun phrase, so the reader reaches the marker
   before knowing what follows it.
3. The marker sits between two commas mid-sentence (t16), where Turkish
   would place `mesela` at the front.

Not wanted: `for instance` against `for example`; an item where the
"restatement" quietly adds a fact, which would make both readings
defensible.

|---|---|---|---|

# 5 · `Time & Sequence: Meanwhile / Subsequently / By Then`

## 1 · The discrimination

A learner who has this right can place a second event on a timeline
relative to the first: **overlapping it** (`meanwhile`), **following it**
(`subsequently`), or **already complete at a point the text has already
named** (`by then`) — and knows that `by then` needs that named point to
exist in the passage.

**The honest bound.** `meanwhile` and `in the meantime` are one form, as
are `subsequently`, `afterwards` and `later on`, and `by then` and `by
that time`. Nor can this category key on *how long* something took, or on
narrative style. It keys on overlap versus sequence versus
anteriority-to-a-named-point, and nothing else.

## 2 · Misconceptions

- **M20.** Turkish `bu arada` means both *meanwhile* and *by the way*, so
  `meanwhile` is used as a topic-shifter → chosen for a sentence that
  simply changes subject, with no overlap.
- **M21.** Turkish `o zamana kadar` covers both *by then* and *until
  then* → `until then` chosen where the sentence reports the state
  reached at that point, and vice versa. This is the sharpest
  Turkish-specific trap in the topic and t19 is built on it.
- **M22.** `By then` is used with no antecedent time point, as a general
  "then" → chosen in passages that never name a moment.
- **M23.** Any two events reported in two sentences are assumed to be
  sequential, because that is the default reading of a narrative →
  `Subsequently` chosen for simultaneous events.
- **M24.** A time marker is chosen for a relation that is not temporal at
  all (M0 in this category's clothing) → `In short` / `Above all` /
  `For instance` in a timeline slot.

## 3 · The item plan

| # | keys | tests | decided by | misconceptions punished |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| t17 | `Meanwhile` | overlap | meaning — one afternoon holds both actions, and the reason the second could not wait is given | M23, M22, M24 |
| t18 | `Subsequently` | sequence | meaning — the sweep *corrects* the earlier dives, so it followed them | M20, M22, M24 |
| t19 | `by then` | anteriority to a named point — **the caveat item** | meaning — "a little after eleven" is the antecedent; `until then` is the Turkish trap | M21, M20 |
| t20 | `meanwhile` | overlap, parenthetical mid-sentence position | meaning — the stitching continues through the stoppage | M22, M23, M24 |

Both halves keyed (overlap t17, t20; sequence/anteriority t18, t19), and
all four decided by meaning. t19 exists because `by then` is the member
of the triad Turkish gives no reliable handle on.

## 4 · Context bank

1. Surgery on an eagle's wing while a nest is searched for. *(t17)*
2. A wreck survey corrected by a sonar sweep. *(t18)*
3. A rescue helicopter grounded by fog. *(t19)*
4. A print works with stopped presses and hand binders. *(t20)*
5. A power cut during a live radio broadcast.
6. A relay swimmer waiting on the block.
7. A greenhouse boiler failing overnight in February.
8. A customs hold on a consignment of vaccines.

## 5 · Difficulty recipe

1. The named time point that licenses `by then` is in the *previous*
   sentence (t19).
2. The sequence has to be inferred from a consequence ("both logs had to
   be rewritten") rather than from a date.
3. The marker sits between commas, where the learner's ear expects a
   sentence-initial adverb (t20).

Not wanted: `meanwhile` against `in the meantime`; a passage with two
dates in it, which turns the item into arithmetic.

## 6 · Coverage ledger

| form | key | distractor | absent |
|---|---|---|---|
| `meanwhile` | t17, t20 | t18, t19 — and t3, t5, t9, t22, t24 in other categories | |

# 6 · `Emphasis & Summary: Above All / In Short / Overall`

## 1 · The discrimination

A learner who has this right can tell three closing moves apart:
**singling out the most important item from several already listed**
(`above all`), **compressing what was just said, adding nothing**
(`in short`), and **weighing mixed evidence into a verdict**
(`overall`).

**The honest bound.** `in short`, `in brief` and `to sum up` are one
form, as are `overall`, `on the whole` and `all in all`. Worse, `in
short` and `overall` genuinely overlap wherever a final sentence both
compresses *and* judges — so **no item in this topic puts `in short` and
`overall` in the same option list.** Each is keyed against `above all`,
which is decidable (is one item being ranked above the others, or not?),
and against markers from other functions.

## 2 · Misconceptions

- **M25.** Turkish `her şeyden önce` means both *above all* and *first of
  all*, so `above all` is read as a sequence marker → `First of all`
  chosen for the item the text ranks highest; t24 springs this.
- **M26.** `In short` is treated as a general closer and used where the
  final sentence adds a new item → chosen for an emphasis slot.
- **M27.** `Overall` is read as "in general / genellikle", a
  generalisation about typical cases, rather than as a verdict on this
  case.
- **M28.** Any final sentence is assumed to be a summary, so the evaluative
  or additive work it does goes unread → `In short` for a verdict,
  `Above all` for a compression.
- **M29.** A summary marker is chosen for a relation that is not
  summary at all (M0 again) → `Similarly` / `Meanwhile` / `For instance`
  in a closing slot.

## 3 · The item plan

| # | keys | tests | decided by | misconceptions punished |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| t21 | `Above all` | emphasis — a fourth duty ranked over three listed | meaning | M26, M28, M29 |
| t22 | `In short` | summary — four steps compressed to one consequence, no new fact | meaning | M28, M26 (reversed), M29 |
| t23 | `Overall` | verdict — two negatives and one positive weighed | meaning | M28, M27, M17 (`In other words`) |
| t24 | `Above all` | emphasis — **the caveat item**: `above all` ≠ `first of all` | meaning — "that question settled it" ranks it, and it comes *after* the other three | M25, M26 |

Both halves keyed (emphasis t21, t24; summary/verdict t22, t23), all four
by meaning. `In short` and `Overall` never meet, per the bound.

## 4 · Context bank

1. A sleeper-train attendant's duties. *(t21)*
2. A university form and four countersignatures. *(t22)*
3. A new tram line's first year. *(t23)*
4. A panel judging bridge designs. *(t24)*
5. A hill farm's accounts after a dry summer.
6. A neighbourhood library's move to a smaller building.
7. A dictionary revised over eleven years.
8. A hospital's new triage system after six months.

## 5 · Difficulty recipe

1. The ranking is carried by a clause after the blank ("and that question
   settled it"), not by the marker itself (t24).
2. The passage's evidence is genuinely mixed, so the verdict is not
   predictable from any single sentence (t23).
3. The list before the blank is long enough that the reader has to decide
   whether the closing sentence is *inside* it or *about* it.

Not wanted: `in short` against `overall`; a closing sentence that would
serve as either a summary or a verdict; `to sum up` offered beside
`in short`.

**Deliberate absences, for the lesson author.** `furthermore`,
`in addition`, `besides`, `nonetheless`, `consequently`, `hence`,
`so as to`, `in order that`, `for example`, `that is`, `such as`,
`in the meantime`, `afterwards`, `by that time`, `on the whole`,
`all in all`, `to sum up` are all taught-worthy and **appear in no option
list**. That is not an oversight and not a hole in the §6 sense: each is
a register twin of a marker that *is* keyed, and the kickoff's honest
bound forbids an item that separates twins. Teach them explicitly as
equivalents — "these are the same choice, and the exam will not ask you
to rank them" — so the learner is not left waiting for a question that
never comes.

**Two markers this topic deliberately leaves to `closest-meaning`:**
`despite` and `although`. They belong to that topic's *Despite vs
Although vs However* lesson, and offering them here would put the same
teaching in two places in the index.
