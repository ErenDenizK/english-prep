## What this topic is, and why its categories look like this

Every other topic in the app teaches a form. This one teaches a
**judgement**: given a sentence and four fluent paraphrases, decide which
one means the same thing. The grammar is the vehicle, not the subject.

So each category is named for the structure its four items turn on — that
is what lets a wrong answer link to a lesson, which is how the whole app
works — but every lesson has the same second job: **name the move the
distractors make.** From `docs/exam-spec.md`, the paper's own distractors
are grammatically fluent and wrong about meaning, in five recurring ways:

| Move | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| **Reverse** | swaps cause and effect, or which of two things is compared |
| **Re-modalise** | a certainty becomes a possibility, an obligation becomes advice |
| **Re-time** | an unfulfilled past becomes an open future; a finished result becomes a prediction |
| **Drop a condition** | states flatly what the original made conditional |
| **Overshoot** | true, but says more than the original does |

Every distractor in this topic must be one of these five, named by number
in the item plan. A distractor that is wrong because it is
*ungrammatical* is not a restatement distractor at all — it is a cloze
option that wandered in, and the learner discards it without reading.

## The six categories

### 1 · `Third Conditional vs Mixed Conditional`

**Discrimination.** Can tell an unfulfilled past from its consequence:
whether the result is also in the past (*would have reached*) or in the
present (*would be there now*).

**Honest bound.** Do not key on second-vs-third where the sentence gives
no time anchor — *if I knew* and *if I had known* paraphrase each other
freely in a vacuum.

**Misconceptions.** M1 the third conditional describes something that
*did* happen. M2 *would have* is a past tense of *will*. M3 the mixed
conditional's present result can be written in the past.

**Item plan.** ① third → past result, punishing M1. ② mixed → present
result, punishing M3. ③ inverted *Had she left…*, punishing M2.
④ the reverse direction: a plain past statement restated *as* a
conditional.

### 2 · `Unless vs If Not vs Otherwise`

**Discrimination.** Can convert a negative condition without flipping the
polarity of the wrong clause.

**Honest bound.** `unless` and `if not` are not interchangeable in
every sentence, and the cases where they diverge are too subtle for this
level. Key only where both would work and the *rest* of the paraphrase is
what decides.

**Misconceptions.** M4 `unless` = `if`. M5 `otherwise` attaches to the
condition rather than to the consequence. M6 double negatives cancel
silently.

### 3 · `Despite vs Although vs However`

**Discrimination.** Can move a concession between a preposition, a
conjunction and an adverb without losing which clause is the concession.

**Honest bound.** *Despite* + noun and *although* + clause say the same
thing; do not key on which is more natural. Key on **which clause is
conceded**, which is what a reversed distractor gets wrong.

**Misconceptions.** M7 *despite* takes a clause. M8 the concession and
the main point are interchangeable. M9 *however* joins two clauses like
*but*.

### 4 · `As...As vs Comparatives vs The More...The More`

**Discrimination.** Can restate a comparison without swapping which side
is greater. This is where the paper's Reverse move lives most often.

**Honest bound.** Do not key on *less advanced than* versus *not as
advanced as* — they are equivalent. Key on **direction** and on the
correlative structure.

**Misconceptions.** M10 *not as X as* means *more X than*. M11 the
correlative's two clauses can be swapped. M12 *the more* takes a
superlative.

### 5 · `Too vs Enough vs So...That`

**Discrimination.** Can tell a degree that **prevents** a result from a
degree that **produces** one.

**Honest bound.** *too heavy to lift* and *so heavy that nobody could
lift it* are equivalent; key on the negative/positive flip, not on
register.

**Misconceptions.** M13 *too* = *very*. M14 *enough* precedes its
adjective. M15 *so…that* and *such…that* take the same complement.

### 6 · `Passive Reporting: It Is Said vs Is Said To`

**Discrimination.** Can move between the impersonal and personal
reporting patterns without inventing a source or losing the time
relation (*is said to be* against *is said to have been*).

**Honest bound.** The two patterns are genuine paraphrases, so an item
cannot be keyed on which is better. Key on the **time relation** and on
distractors that name a source the original withheld — the Overshoot
move.

**Misconceptions.** M16 the two patterns can be blended (*is said that he
to be*). M17 *is said to be* and *is said to have been* are
interchangeable. M18 an unnamed source may be supplied in a paraphrase.

This category already has a lesson elsewhere in the app
(`passive-voice`), which teaches the **forms**. This one teaches
restating them, and its lesson must not repeat that one's example
sentences — the two will sit next to each other in a mixed test.

may reuse a sentence from its own lesson.

## Difficulty recipe

1. The distractor is true of the world but not of the sentence.
2. The deciding word is in a subordinate clause, not the main one.
3. The paraphrase is longer than the original, so the reader has to hold
   both.

Not wanted: rare vocabulary, an ungrammatical option, or a choice between
two accurate paraphrases.
