## What this topic is, and the one thing that makes it hard

Turkish has **one** verbal noun. `-mAk` and `-mA` cover everything
English splits between `to do` and `doing`, and they take case endings
rather than changing shape: *gitmeyi düşündüm*, *gitmek istedim*,
*gitmekten korktum*. So the learner arrives with no intuition at all for
the distinction — not a wrong intuition, **none** — and fills the gap
with three invented rules that this topic's distractors are built out of:

- `to` is the future/purpose particle, so a forward-looking complement
  takes `to`;
- `-ing` belongs to the continuous tenses, so a gerund after a past-tense
  verb looks like a tense error;
- `make`/`let` take a bare infinitive, so any verb with no visible `to`
  takes the bare form.

Every misconception numbered below is one of those three wearing a
different coat, or a lexical transfer from a specific Turkish verb.
**Misconceptions are numbered globally (M1–M31) across the topic**, so a
distractor in category 5 can cite the overcorrection category 4 creates.
That happens twice and both are deliberate.

**The topic-wide honest bound.** English tolerates both forms after
`begin`, `start`, `continue`, `like`, `love`, `hate`, `prefer`, `intend`,
`bother` and (in most contexts) `help`, with no difference a B2 learner
should be marked on. **No item in this topic is keyed on any of those
verbs**, in any category. They are the topic's D1 factory.

# 1 · `Verb + Gerund vs Verb + Infinitive`

## 1 · The discrimination

A learner who has this right can name, for a frequent verb, which
complement it takes — and does not reach for the form that matches the
*meaning* of the sentence (forward-looking → `to`, ongoing → `-ing`),
because English does not work that way here.

**The honest bound.** This is a memory list, not a rule. There is no
generalisation under `avoid + -ing` and `manage + to` that a learner can
derive; an item here tests whether they have met the verb. So:

- the verbs are kept to the frequent core — `enjoy`, `avoid`, `suggest`,
  `deny`, `decide`, `manage`, `refuse`, `admit`;
- **nothing is keyed on a both-forms verb** (see the topic-wide bound),
  and nothing on a verb whose two complements differ in meaning — that is
  category 2's material and putting it here is D9;
- the reasoning load in this topic sits in categories 2, 3 and 5, and
  this category's job is coverage, not difficulty. Two of its four items
  therefore make the *lexical* choice (which verb the context calls for)
  carry the item, so that it is not four memory recalls in a row.

## 2 · Misconceptions

  M1. The two English forms are free variants, because Turkish uses one
      verbal noun for both → picks by ear; whichever reads more smoothly.
  M2. `suggest` = *önermek*, and Turkish says *taşımamızı önerdi*, so the
      complement is read as person + infinitive → *suggested them to
      move*, *suggested to move*.
  M3. `make`/`let` take a bare infinitive, so a verb with no visible `to`
      takes the bare form → *suggest move*, *got him go*.
  M4. `-ing` is a continuous-tense marker, so a gerund after a past-tense
      verb is a tense error to be repaired → *refused paying* "fixed" to
      *refused to pay* by luck, or *avoids driving* "fixed" the wrong way
      to *avoids to drive*.
  M5. `to` means purpose or futurity, so a complement pointing forward in
      time takes `to` regardless of the verb → *avoids to drive*,
      *suggested to move*.
  M6. A stated refusal and a quiet habit are the same thing, so `refuse`
      is available anywhere the sentence feels negative → *refuses to
      drive* where the text says no rule was ever announced.

## 3 · The item plan

| # | keys | tests | decided by | misconceptions punished |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| t1 | `avoids driving` | gerund half; *avoid* vs *refuse* | meaning (habit vs declared refusal) + form | M4, M5, M6 |
| t2 | `managed to repair` | infinitive half; *manage* vs *enjoy* | meaning (difficulty overcome) + form | M1, M4 |
| t3 | `suggested moving` | gerund half, forward-looking complement | signal (the verb) | M2, M3, M5 |
| t4 | `refused to pay` | infinitive half; *refuse* vs *admit* | meaning (withholding vs confessing) + form | M1, M4, M6 |

Both halves keyed 2:2. Three of four are decided by meaning as well as by
the verb, which is what stops the category being four flashcards. t3 is
the rule-punisher: the complement of `suggest` looks entirely forward in
time, which is exactly where M5 says `to` must go, and it takes `-ing`.

## 5 · Difficulty recipe

1. The context, not the verb, decides which of two verbs is meant, and
   both verbs appear in the options with both complements.
2. The complement points forward in time while the verb takes `-ing`.
3. The deciding clause sits after the blank.

Not wanted: a rare verb, a both-forms verb, or an option that is wrong
only because it is less idiomatic.

# 2 · `Both, With a Meaning Change: Remember / Stop / Try / Regret`

## 1 · The discrimination

A learner who has this right can read a paragraph, work out **which event
happened and in what order**, and pick the complement that says so:
`remember doing` looks back at an act already performed, `remember to do`
looks forward at a task; `stop doing` ends an activity, `stop to do`
interrupts one in order to begin another; `try doing` experiments with a
method, `try to do` attempts something difficult; `regret doing` wishes an
act undone, `regret to do` delivers bad news as it is being delivered.

**The honest bound — and it is the whole category.** The two forms are
**both correct English**. Nothing in the option list decides between
them; only the paragraph does. So an item here is broken unless the text
settles the time relation *before* the blank is reached, and "settles"
means a reader can point at the clause that does it. Three tests, applied
to every draft in this category:

- delete the clause you think decides it — if the item still has one
  answer, that clause was not what was deciding, and the real decider may
  not exist;
- read the paragraph with the *other* form substituted and ask whether it
  describes a possible world. If it does, the item has two answers;
- never negate the frame. Under negation `forget` and `remember` swap
  direction (*he did not forget to lock it* asserts the act happened),
  and no amount of context recovers it.

Also out of bounds: the perfect gerund. `remember having locked` is a
correct paraphrase of `remember locking`, so it is a D1 waiting to be
offered, and it appears in no option list in this topic.

## 2 · Misconceptions

  M7. Turkish marks neither difference (*kilitlediğimi hatırladım* /
      *kilitlemeyi hatırladım* are both natural, and the second is not
      restricted to the future), so the pair reads as one meaning → picks
      the familiar form.
  M8. The infinitive is the neutral, unmarked form; `-ing` is the special
      one → `remember to lock` used for recall.
  M9. `stop` is memorised as a gerund verb ("stop smoking"), so
      `stop + to` is read as an error rather than as the other pattern.
  M10. `try to` = *denemek* covers both English senses, so the
      experiment reading is never reached → `try to use` for a method
      swap that cost no effort at all.
  M11. `regret` is bundled with `decide`/`hope` as a to-verb → *regret to
      sell*; or the announcement use is not known at all, so `regret to
      inform` looks like a mistake.
  M12. The four verbs are interchangeable inside the category, because
      they were learnt as one list → `remembered` where the text says
      nothing about memory.

## 3 · The item plan

| # | keys | tests | decided by | misconceptions punished |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| t5 | `remembered leaving` | gerund = recall of a completed act | meaning (a remembered image, no task was ever set) | M7, M8, M12 |
| t6 | `stopped to fill` | infinitive = interruption for a purpose | meaning (the activity had not been going on) | M9, M10 |
| t7 | `tried using` | gerund = experiment with a method | meaning (it was effortless and it worked) | M10, M12 |
| t8 | `regret to inform` | infinitive = the announcement being made now | meaning (the bad news is in the sentence) | M11, M12 |

Keys 2:2 gerund/infinitive, one item per verb. **Every one is decided by
meaning**; there is no signal word in this category and there must not be
one, because a signal word here would be a lie. t6 and t8 are the
rule-punishers: t6 keys the form the learner's rule forbids, and t8 keys
the exception that makes `regret` behave unlike the other three.

Each item's option list is a 2×2 of two of the four verbs against both
complements, so all four options are grammatical and only the paragraph
separates them. That shape is also what keeps a wrong answer diagnostic:
picking the same verb's other form means the time relation was misread,
picking the other verb means the event was.

## 4 · Context bank

1. A witness recalling an envelope left on a hall table.
2. Cyclists at a fountain below a mountain pass.
3. A lecture-theatre projector and a technician swapping sockets.
4. A dean's letter refusing an appeal.
5. *(spare)* A driver who pulls over at a lay-by.
6. *(spare)* A gardener alternating two remedies for blight.
7. *(spare)* A family who sold a flat above a bakery too cheaply.
8. *(spare)* A conductor who cut a repeat from a symphony.

## 5 · Difficulty recipe

1. The clause that settles the time relation is not adjacent to the
   blank — it is in the previous sentence, or the one after.
2. The evidence is physical rather than stated: footage, a receipt, a
   full bottle, a picture the witness can still see.
3. Both verbs in the 2×2 are plausible until one clause rules one out.

Not wanted: leaving the relation open and defending the key as "more
likely"; that is D1, and in this category it is the only real risk.

# 3 · `Causative Verb Patterns: Make / Let / Have / Get`

## 1 · The discrimination

A learner who has this right can pick the complement from the **verb**,
not from the meaning, because all four verbs mean roughly "cause someone
to do something" and none of them takes the same shape:

| | pattern |
|---|---|
| `make` | `make + object + bare infinitive` |
| `let` | `let + object + bare infinitive` |
| `get` | `get + object + **to** infinitive` |
| `have` | `have + person + bare infinitive` / `have + **thing** + past participle` |

Four rules, and `have` is two of them. This is the most exam-relevant
category in the topic: the sample paper's third cloze blank is a
`make + object + bare infinitive` (`docs/exam-spec.md`).

**The honest bound.** `make`, `get` and `have` are not
interchangeable in meaning — force, persuasion, arrangement — but the
edges are soft, and an item keyed on *which* is more natural where two
would work is D3. So: an item is keyed on the **form** wherever two
verbs would both fit the meaning, and keyed on the **meaning** only where
the context rules the other verb out flatly (a boy who has been begging
all summer is not being forced). Never both softly.

Also bounded: `allow` and `permit`. `allowed him to drive` is a correct
paraphrase of `let him drive`, so neither appears in any option list.

## 2 · Misconceptions

  M13. `to` is the mark of a verbal complement in English, so it belongs
       after any causative → *make somebody to do*, *let somebody to do*,
       *have somebody to do*. This is the single most common error this
       audience makes, and the lesson is built on it.
  M14. The bare-infinitive rule learnt for `make`/`let` is then
       generalised to `get` → *got him go*.
  M15. `have something done` is read as a present perfect, so the past
       participle is heard as a tense and the order collapses →
       *had reprinted them* for *had them reprinted*.
  M16. A thing can be the agent of a causative, because Turkish
       *bastırdı* needs no animate object → *had them reprint* for
       photographs that reprint themselves.
  M17. Permission and compulsion are not distinguished, because Turkish
       marks both with the causative suffix → `made` where the text says
       the boy had been asking for months.
  M18. `get` is only "obtain", so the persuasion sense is not available
       → the learner reaches for `make` when the object could not
       possibly be forced.

## 3 · The item plan

| # | keys | tests | decided by | misconceptions punished |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| t9 | `made us read` | `make` + bare infinitive — the paper's own blank | signal (the verb) + meaning (compulsion) | M13, M14 |
| t10 | `got him to go` | `get` + **to** infinitive; *get* vs *let* | meaning (persuasion over weeks, not permission) | M13, M14, M17, M18 |
| t11 | `had them reprinted` | `have` + **thing** + past participle | meaning (the photographs cannot reprint) | M15, M16 |
| t12 | `let him drive` | `let` + bare infinitive; *let* vs *make* | meaning (he had been asking since June) | M13, M17 |

Two bare-infinitive keys, one `to`-infinitive key, one past-participle
key — all four rules keyed, which is the point of a category whose name
lists four verbs. Three of four are decided by meaning; t9 is the one
decided by the verb, and that is allowed once (D4 is "fatal as a
pattern", not once).

t11 is the rule-punisher. A learner who has taken `have + object + bare
infinitive` from the lesson gets it wrong, and that is precisely the
caveat the lesson's most emphatic block should carry.

## 4 · Context bank

1. A literature teacher who drilled a poem aloud, one line each.
2. A grandfather who refused to see a doctor for two years.
3. Faded photographs in a barber's window, and the old negatives.
4. A boy allowed to drive the tractor as far as the gate.
5. *(spare)* A landlord and a broken extractor fan.
6. *(spare)* A ferry captain and the last two foot passengers.
7. *(spare)* A conductor who kept the orchestra past the hour.
8. *(spare)* A tailor, a wedding suit and four days.

## 5 · Difficulty recipe

1. The verb is not named anywhere in the paragraph — the relation
   (force, permission, persuasion, arrangement) has to be read out of the
   events.
2. The object is a thing rather than a person, so `have` changes shape.
3. The wrong-form option is the form a *different* causative genuinely
   takes, so it is a real English pattern in the wrong place.

Not wanted: a choice between `make` and `have` where both would be
accepted, or between `let` and `allow`.

## 6 · Coverage ledger

| form | key | distractor | absent |
|---|---|---|---|
| `make + O + bare inf` | t9 | | |
| `let + O + bare inf` | t12 | | |
| `get + O + to inf` | t10 | | |
| `have + thing + past participle` | t11 | | |
| `have + person + bare inf` | | t11 (as *made them reprint*'s foil) | see note |
| `make + O + to inf` (M13) | | t9, t10, t12 | |
| `let + O + to inf` (M13) | | t12 | |
| `have + O + to inf` (M13) | | t9, t12 | |
| `get + O + bare inf` (M14) | | t9, t10 | |
| `have + past participle + O` (M15) | | t11 | |

Two notes, both for the supervisor rather than for the lesson author to
paper over:

- `have + person + bare infinitive` is keyed nowhere. Four items and four
  rules leave no room, and the thing-plus-participle pattern is the one
  the exam rewards, so it took the slot. A fifth item would fix it.
- **`be made to + infinitive` is the hole.** The passive of `make` takes
  `to` — *we were made to wait* — which is the one place M13 is correct,
  and no item in this category can reach it: every frame here is active,
  and a passive distractor cannot be offered where the subject is the
  causer. If the lesson teaches it, it is an L5 untested caveat by
  construction. The recommendation is that the lesson teach it as
  recognition only, flagged as such, until a fifth item exists.

# 4 · `Adjective + Infinitive vs Preposition + Gerund`

## 1 · The discrimination

A learner who has this right knows that what follows decides the shape:
after an **adjective standing alone** the complement is `to + infinitive`
(*reluctant to sign*, *quick to point out*); after a **preposition** —
whatever the preposition, and whoever it belongs to — the complement is
`-ing` (*capable of singing*, *responsible for checking*, *looking
forward to seeing*).

**The honest bound.** Several adjectives take both, with a real or an
invisible difference, and none of them may be keyed:

- `interested to hear` (keen to receive news) and `interested in
  hearing` are both current English;
- `afraid to ask` and `afraid of asking` differ too finely for B2;
- `sorry to keep you waiting` and `sorry for keeping you waiting` are
  both idiomatic in the same situation;
- `able to do` and `capable of doing` are synonyms in different shapes,
  so an option list must never offer the correct form of both.

What is left, and what this category is keyed on, is: adjectives that
take only the infinitive, adjectives welded to one preposition, and the
`to` that is a preposition.

## 2 · Misconceptions

  M19. Turkish attaches a case ending to the verbal noun
       (*imzalamakta isteksiz*), so a complement after an adjective is
       expected to carry something → a preposition is inserted where
       English has none: *reluctant of handing*, *quick at pointing*.
  M20. `to` is always the infinitive marker → *looking forward to see*,
       *object to pay*. This is the category's centre.
  M21. Having learnt M20's correction, the learner overcorrects and puts
       `-ing` after every `to` → *decided to going*. (Cited by a
       distractor in category 1, and by one in category 5.)
  M22. `capable` is filed with `able`, which takes `to` → *capable to
       sing*; and symmetrically *able of singing*.
  M23. A preposition can be followed by the bare verb, because Turkish
       does not need an inflected form there → *capable of sing*,
       *responsible for check*.
  M24. `for` translates *için* and therefore marks any complement of
       purpose or duty → *quick for pointing*, *reluctant for handing*.

## 3 · The item plan

| # | keys | tests | decided by | misconceptions punished |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| t13 | `reluctant to hand` | adjective + infinitive; *reluctant* vs *eager* | meaning (eleven weeks and a photograph) + form | M19, M24 |
| t14 | `capable of singing` | adjective + fixed preposition + gerund | signal (the adjective) | M22, M23 |
| t15 | `looking forward to seeing` | the `to` that is a preposition | signal, and it is the trap | M20, M23, M24 |
| t16 | `quick to point` | adjective + infinitive; *quick* vs *slow* | meaning (within an hour, and the vote lost) | M19, M24 |

Keys 2:2 infinitive/gerund. t13 and t16 are decided by meaning as well as
form — the context has to say which adjective before the shape matters.
t15 is the rule-punisher, and it is the highest-value item in the
category: the learner's rule ("`to` means infinitive") is not merely
insufficient here, it is actively wrong, and this is the one place in the
topic where `to + -ing` is the key.

## 4 · Context bank

1. A landlord withholding a deposit for eleven weeks.
2. A singer who stumbled in rehearsal and sang the part from memory.
3. A return from two years abroad, walking up from the station.
4. A committee, a footnoted survey and a lost vote.
5. *(spare)* An engineer who will not sign off a bridge.
6. *(spare)* A night porter answerable for the keys.
7. *(spare)* A translator objecting to a publisher's cut.
8. *(spare)* A swimmer nervous about the crossing.

## 5 · Difficulty recipe

1. The blank follows a `to` that is a preposition, in a sentence where
   an infinitive would also read smoothly to a careless eye.
2. The adjective is not given — the context decides which of two
   opposite adjectives is meant, and each is offered in both shapes.
3. The distractor is the *correct* shape of a near-synonymous adjective.

Not wanted: an adjective that takes both, or a preposition choice
(`good at` vs `good in`) with no verb-form consequence — that is
vocabulary, not this category.

## 6 · Coverage ledger

|---|---|---|---|

| adjective + preposition + `-ing` | t14 | t13, t16 | |
| preposition `to` + `-ing` | t15 | | |

# 5 · `Infinitive of Purpose vs For + Gerund`

## 1 · The discrimination

A learner who has this right can express *why somebody did something*
with `to + infinitive` — never `for` — and still use `for + -ing` where a
preposition genuinely governs the verb.

The line the exam draws:

| the sentence says | form |
|---|---|
| why an agent performed an action | `to + infinitive` (`in order to`, `so as to`) |
| the verb is governed by a preposition (`fined for`, `an apology for`, `thank … for`) | `for + -ing` |
| a purpose named as a thing | `for + noun` (*he came for the conference*) |

**The honest bound, and it is severe.** Where a purpose attaches to an
**object** rather than to an action, `for + -ing` and `to + infinitive`
are both correct — *a machine for washing clothes* / *a machine to wash
clothes*, *this file is used for sharpening* / *used to sharpen*. That is
half of what textbooks put in this category and **none of it may be
keyed**. Both `for + -ing` keys in this topic therefore sit where a
preposition is structurally required — after a verb that subcategorises
for `for`, and after a noun that does — so the infinitive is not merely
less good, it is ungrammatical.

## 2 · Misconceptions

  M25. *için* is glossed as `for`, so purpose is written with `for` →
       *for putting their names on a list*.
  M26. *için* is glossed as `for` **and** the verb still needs the
       infinitive marker → *for to put*. The classic calque, and it
       survives well into B2 in written work.
  M27. A preposition can govern a bare verb → *for put*, *for flatten*.
  M28. The overcorrection from category 4 (M21): having learnt that some
       `to`s take `-ing`, the learner writes `to + -ing` for purpose →
       *to putting*.
  M29. `to + infinitive` after a verb of penalty or thanks is read as
       the purpose of *that* verb → *the council fined it to block the
       footway*, i.e. the council wanted the footway blocked.
  M30. `in order to` is thought of as a more formal `for`, so it is
       substituted into a slot where no purpose is being expressed at
       all.

## 3 · The item plan

| # | keys | tests | decided by | misconceptions punished |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| t17 | `to put` | purpose of an action | meaning (a `for + noun` decoy is refuted in the same sentence) | M25, M26, M28 |
| t18 | `for blocking` | verb governing `for` (`fined … for`) | meaning (the council did not want the footway blocked) | M29, M30, M26 |
| t19 | `to read` | purpose of an action, with `for + noun` correct earlier in the text | meaning | M25, M26, M28 |
| t20 | `for flattening` | noun governing `for` (`an apology for`) | signal (the noun) | M26, M27, M25 |

Keys 2:2. t18 is the rule-punisher: the infinitive is offered in two
guises (`to block`, `in order to block`) and both are the reading a
purpose-hunting learner reaches for, and both are wrong because the `for`
belongs to `fined`, not to the café.

The option lists in this category repeat their **shape** — `to V` /
`for V-ing` / `for to V` / a fourth error — across all four items. That
is deliberate and is not D11: the shape is the category, the real exam
prints exactly this, and the items differ in which of the four is right
and in why. What must not repeat is the scenario, and it does not.

8. *(spare)* A council permit for felling a lime tree.

## 5 · Difficulty recipe

1. A correct `for + noun` appears elsewhere in the paragraph, so the
   learner's eye is trained on the wrong word.
2. The governing verb or noun that requires `for` is separated from the
   blank by an object.
3. The infinitive distractor produces a *coherent but different*
   sentence, so the item punishes a plausible misreading rather than a
   guess.

Not wanted: a purpose attached to an object, where both forms are
correct; that is the bound above and it is where this category goes wrong.

# 6 · `Used To vs Be Used To vs Get Used To`

## 1 · The discrimination

A learner who has this right can tell three unrelated things apart that
share five letters:

| | means | complement |
|---|---|---|
| `used to + infinitive` | a past habit or state that has ended | bare verb |
| `be used to + -ing/noun` | is accustomed to it now | gerund or noun |
| `get used to + -ing/noun` | is becoming accustomed — a process | gerund or noun |

**The honest bound.** `didn't use to` / `didn't used to` is a live
spelling dispute in real English and is not keyed here; the exam's
register prefers the first, but marking a learner wrong for the second
punishes someone who reads modern English. Nor is anything keyed on
`would` for past habits — it overlaps `used to` for repeated actions and
diverges only for states, which is a different lesson.

The one place `used to` and `be used to` genuinely collide — a repeated
past action that the subject was also accustomed to — is not keyable
either. Both items in that region are therefore built on frames that rule
one form out **structurally**: an existential *there* (which cannot be
accustomed to anything) and an explicit process adverbial (which a state
verb cannot take).

## 2 · Misconceptions

  M31. All three are the same expression, because Turkish uses
       *-erdi* for the past habit and *alışkın/alışmak* for the other
       two without any shared form → picks whichever is shortest.
  M32. `be used to` is `used to` with a tense on it → *was used to
       being a cinema* for *there used to be a cinema*.
  M33. `to` in `be used to` is the infinitive marker → *is used to
       sleep*, *got used to hear*. The signature error of the category.
  M34. State and process are not distinguished, since Turkish
       *alışmıştım* covers both → `was used to` where the text says the
       accustoming took months.
  M35. `used` in `be used to` is never the passive of *use* → the
       learner rejects the correct *was used to open the cabinet by the
       assistant* on sight.
  M36. `used to` has a present form → *he uses to walk*.

## 3 · The item plan

| # | keys | tests | decided by | misconceptions punished |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| t21 | `used to be` | past state, now over | meaning + structure (existential *there* blocks the other two) | M31, M32 |
| t22 | `is used to sleeping` | present accustomed state | meaning (the habit continues; the light does not bother her) | M31, M33 |
| t23 | `got used to hearing` | the process of becoming accustomed | meaning (*little by little* forces a process verb) | M33, M34 |
| t24 | `was used to open` | the passive of *use* — the homograph | meaning + structure (a `by`-agent blocks the habit reading) | M33, M35 |

All three named forms are keyed, and t24 keys the fourth thing that wears
the same clothes. t24 is the rule-punisher, and the strongest item in the
category: a learner who has taken "`be used to` is always followed by
`-ing`" from the lesson gets it wrong, which is why the lesson has to say
that the rule holds only when `used` means *accustomed*.

Every item is decided by meaning; two of them additionally have a
structural block, which is what keeps them out of the D1 region the
honest bound describes.

## 4 · Context bank

1. A mother giving directions by landmarks that are gone.
2. Eleven years of night shifts.
3. A first month in a flat above a bus station.
4. A brass key in a chemist's, before the locks were changed.
5. *(spare)* A returning emigrant and the price of bread.
6. *(spare)* A left-hand-drive car on the first week abroad.
7. *(spare)* A vegetarian's first month of cooking.
8. *(spare)* A tram line where the river used to run.

## 5 · Difficulty recipe

1. The paragraph's tense elsewhere, not the blank, says whether the
   state is over — a present-tense continuation kills `used to`.
2. A process adverbial (*little by little*, *by the third month*) forces
   `get` over `be`, and it is the only thing that does.
3. A `by`-agent turns the whole expression into a passive.

Not wanted: an item resting on `didn't use to`'s spelling, on `would`,
or on a context where the subject both habitually did something and was
accustomed to doing it.

## Topic-wide notes for the lesson author

1. **Do not build a lesson example on any scenario in an assigned row of
   a context bank above** (rows 1–4 of each). `check` blocks draw from
   this category's own questions, so a lesson example that matches one
   makes the check a recall of the block three screens up.
2. Category 3 has a named hole (`be made to`) and category 1 has a broad
   one (the memory list). Both are recorded rather than hidden.
3. The `to` in category 4's `look forward to` and in category 6's
   `be used to` is the same preposition. Cross-referencing them is the
   cheapest teaching in the topic, and it is why M21 and M28 exist as a
   pair.
